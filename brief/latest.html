01The five-minute version

The Bank held 6–3 as expected. It then did three other things that mattered more than the vote count.

One. The vote was 6–3. The message was hawkish. The gilt market didn’t care.

Pill, Greene and Mann again voted for a 25bp hike to 4.00% — the same three, the same dissent count as 30 July. Consensus called it right. What consensus did not have was the accompanying language: inflation is now expected to reach slightly over 4% in early 2027, a material upward revision from August’s track; the MPC judges the risks tilted further to the upside than at the July Report; and Bailey’s Committee volunteered the sentence “given the lags with which second-round effects appeared, it was not appropriate to wait too long for evidence of such effects before responding with policy.” That is Warsh-adjacent phrasing under Bailey’s signature. November is now the live meeting.

Two. QT got rewritten. The long end got a rescue.

The Bank paused all active gilt sales for six months and halted long-dated (over 20–year) gilt sales entirely. The new run-off plan takes the Asset Purchase Facility to zero at an average of £46bn/year by end-2034 through £20bn of active sales plus maturities. Prior guidance was a £100bn annual reduction. The gilt market read that as the single most important part of today’s package: the 10-year fell through 5.35% for the first time in over a fortnight, and long-end sensitivity moderated after weeks of 30-year prints north of 5.85%. That eases the mechanical pressure on the Budget’s £13.8bn of fiscal headroom exactly where it was most acute.

Three. Sterling paid the price for the divergence, not for the vote.

Cable traded to 1.3372 during the day, the weakest since late July, and closed near 1.3378. That is a Fed-hiked-BoE-held sequence more than a repricing of British domestic policy: the market thinks the November UK hike probability rose but not enough to close the widened US–UK differential. That is good for GBP-reporting US funds mechanically and bad for anything import-price sensitive over the next quarter.

And the thing that made tomorrow’s NAVs less bad than yesterday’s brief warned

US 10-year Treasury yield eased three basis points to 4.95%, ending eight consecutive days of gains. Semis reversed hard: SOXX +3%, AMD +7%, Broadcom +3%, NVIDIA +2%. The S&P 500 closed at 7,596, +0.59%. The Warsh-terrified market of last night got a Warsh-reassured market this morning — not because Warsh spoke again, but because a Fed that hiked without breaking the ten-year is a Fed the tape can price. Yesterday’s working estimate of £169,500 for tomorrow morning’s book is stale; today’s bounce puts it back closer to £170,300–170,500.

02The BoE hold that wasn’t dovish

A 6–3 hold, a 4%-in-early-2027 inflation forecast, and a QT rewrite that all sit in the same statement.

Break the decision into its three parts and every one of them was hawkish except the headline.

The vote count did what consensus said it would do, but it did not soften. Pill, Greene and Mann again for a hike, the same three, the same 6–3. Zero movement in the dissent count on a day where CPI printed 3.1% with 23% motor-fuel inflation underneath it and yesterday’s Fed delivered its first hike since 2023 is, if anything, a small dovish surprise on the count itself. It is why sterling did not rally on the announcement even as the QT rewrite was pushing gilts higher.

The inflation forecast is the news. The MPC marked its inflation projection up to “slightly over 4%” in early 2027 from the previous track that peaked around 3.7%. That is not a small revision. It is the Bank formally acknowledging that motor-fuel inflation at 23% year on year, food and consumer-goods second-round pass-through, and a currency that has weakened four cents in a month are all combining into a print worse than the August MPR contemplated. Bailey and the doves cannot then, three months from now, meet a 4.1% headline and vote to hold again on the argument that inflation is transitory — the Bank has now written itself out of that defence. The August MPR admitted a “small upside skew” on second-round risk; today’s minutes go significantly further.

The QT rewrite is the surprise. A pause of all active gilt sales for six months, a permanent halt to long-dated (over-20-year) sales, and a new APF run-off pace of £46bn/year through 2034 versus previous guidance of roughly £100bn/year is a material intervention in the long end of the gilt curve. It is not, technically, a monetary policy easing — the Bank was careful in the minutes to frame it as a market-functioning decision rather than a policy stance. But the mechanical effect is that a large marginal seller of long-dated gilts has stepped out of the market for the winter, and the curve responded exactly as one would expect: 10-year yield below 5.35%, long-end sensitivity moderated, and the housing sector on the equity side rallied hard as the mortgage curve got some breathing room.

Read all three together and the picture is a Bank that has decided the mix is: (i) rhetorically prepared to hike in November if the data allows, (ii) unwilling to let the fiscal-monetary tension continue driving 30-year yields toward levels last seen when the Conservatives were still governing, and (iii) content to accept some FX weakness rather than a coordinated hike on the day the Fed delivered. It is the same play the Fed ran twenty-four hours earlier, executed at half the intensity. Bailey does not want to be Warsh; he does want the market to know that Warsh’s inflation frame applies here too.

The homebuilders were the day’s clearest bet on that read. Barratt Redrow +12% is a rounding error away from the largest single-session move in the stock in nearly six years, on results that included an improving reservation rate, a strong order book, and a forecast-beating profit — but the move is bigger than the results because it was overlaid on a lower long-end gilt curve. Vistry, Bellway and Taylor Wimpey all rallied. FTSE 250 closed +1.1%. That is a market that read the QT rewrite the same way the fixed-income desk did.

Where this leaves the sequenceNovember MPC now prices roughly 50% for a 25bp hike, up from ~30% pre-decision. December Fed is 60–70%. If the labour market gives at all in the next four weeks — the Philly Fed print today at 31.3 forecast vs 47.4 prior would say it is — the sequence flips: Warsh gets a downside surprise that lets him talk more moderately at the November FOMC, and the BoE hawks lose the follow-through they need. If it does not give, both banks hike again in November/December respectively, and the current widening in the US–UK differential closes only partially. Your USD lines benefit both ways; the UK domestic sleeve prefers the give.

03Warsh’s day two: the bounce

Yesterday the tape traded the tone. Today the same tape read the rate curve and decided the tone was bark, not bite.

Twenty-four hours ago the Dow was giving back 750 points into and through the press conference, and yesterday’s brief was walking through why the fund sleeve was likely to price ~£500 lower on this morning’s NAVs. That worked out. What did not stay true was the second-order assumption — that Warsh’s hawkish tone would keep the 10-year at or above 5.00% and semis under continued pressure. Both reversed today.

The 10-year is the primary read. Treasury yields fell three basis points on Thursday, taking the 10-year to 4.95% and ending eight consecutive sessions of gains. The 2-year eased to 4.692%, the 30-year to 5.297%. That is a curve saying: the Fed hiked as expected, Warsh signalled more as expected, but the terminal is not moving materially above where it already was. If the market had believed Warsh had genuinely reset the reaction function toward “prepared to do more” without qualification, the 2-year would have moved up 10–15bp today, not down 20bp from yesterday’s peak. It did not.

The semiconductor reaction is the second read. SOXX +3% versus QQQ +2% is a session where the sector-specific rebound outstripped the broad tech bounce. AMD +7%, Broadcom +3% to $349.02, NVIDIA +2%, all on no new stock-specific news. That is a discount-rate trade — you are seeing the same accelerator-demand narrative that took the group to Monday’s AI-pace shock get a valuation reprieve as the ten-year backs off 5%. Not a thesis change, a multiple bump.

The S&P closed +0.59% at 7,596. Coming off a 631-point Dow loss the prior session, this is a partial retracement rather than a fresh leg. But it is enough: the OEIC lines that price into New York’s 4pm close will pick up something in the region of +0.3% to +0.5% on tomorrow’s NAVs, and the direct UK-listed US ETF lines (SMH.L, VUAG, VUSA, SWDA.L) will price the same move. The ATT trim thesis Monday morning has now had two of its three test sessions play out closer to the middle scenario than to the tail: FOMC delivered, Warsh talked, and the sector-composite rebounded on ordinary rate-relief mechanics. If the trim executed at 690p on Monday, this is not yet a reason to reverse, but it is the first session where the alternative — not trimming and running the position through to whoever’s next Q3 call — is holding up better than the middle-scenario the brief was pricing.

The one place the bounce did not reach is the US retail-focused indices. Barrelling into $103 crude on the way down rather than $108 on the way up does very little for the discretionary consumer if the diesel and gasoline prices already in the CPI print need weeks to fade. The August retail sales beat was the print of the week; September’s advance release on 15 October will be the read on whether the impulse persists into a slightly cooler pump-price backdrop.

The pure macro read: yesterday’s tape overreacted to the press conference and today’s tape corrected. Neither is a fundamental change. The fundamental change is Warsh having successfully injected another 25bp of hike probability into the December meeting without breaking the ten-year. That is genuinely a hawkish achievement; the market has just decided it can price it without also selling off the equity complex.

04The energy complex

The Saudi restart briefing that took Brent down $3 yesterday got a follow-up today. Crude at $103, gas at €77, both trending back toward normal — and the read-through is running the CPI transmission in reverse.

Brent settled around $103.61, down 2.10% on the day and now over 4% below Monday’s intraday peak. The proximate move today was Saudi Arabia confirming increased crude routing via Hormuz to compensate for Yanbu-related shortfalls — a re-routing rather than a pipeline restart, but same net effect on physical availability. WTI followed to under $100. Both prints are still comfortably above where they sat at the start of the month, but for the first time in five weeks the direction is clearly the other way.

The refined-product story that turned $100 crude into a 23% motor-fuel CPI reading is what needs to fade for the transmission to reverse. Roughly 800,000 b/d of Russian diesel loss and 1.2 mb/d of Hormuz-disrupted refined products are still off the market; the combined refinery capacity idled is still around 5 mb/d. What today gave the market was an incremental easing of the crude-side pressure, not a resolution of the distillate crack. Petrol pump prices in the UK will follow crude with roughly a two-week lag; if crude sits at $100 rather than $108 through the end of September, the October CPI print (out 21 October) sees the first meaningful moderation in the fuel line. The September CPI print itself, still to come, prices the crude of August — which is what today does not help.

Hormuz flow remains near 7 mb/d against roughly 20 mb/d pre-conflict. That has not changed materially. Iranian permission still required; Tehran still evaluating a service fee. What has changed is that the shipping market has adapted to it: Saudi Arabia rerouting through the strait with Iranian acquiescence, US convoy activity, insurance rates on tanker traffic still elevated but stable rather than climbing. That is a routine background rather than a headline — the sort of state the market prices very differently from an active crisis, even when the underlying number is materially worse than pre-conflict.

For your book, this is another wobble on Shell. London closed at 3,652p going into yesterday’s New York afternoon and gave back on this morning’s open on the Brent settle; then a second down session for Brent today puts a further leg on it. On the standard integrated-major beta the Friday London open re-prices to around 3,570–3,590p if Brent holds around $103. That would take the position from £6,853 to roughly £6,720–6,760, a giveback of £100–130. The five-week run from 3,050p to 3,652p was 20% and is now unwinding at roughly the rate the crude curve is unwinding — not fast, not thesis-breaking, but for the first time not-monotonic.

The refined-product exposure via Fidelity European is still the more consequential book question. TTF drifting through €80 down to €77 removes some of the cost-inflation weight on ASML, Schneider and the rest of the industrial book; a stronger USD partly offsets. On net, Fidelity European gets a mild positive on the industrial cost line and a mild negative on the FX line, and the ECB’s next commentary on cost-push risk is the meta-signal for whether Christine Lagarde’s follow-up-hike option is left on the table.

05Gas and LNG desk view

Standing section. Your own screens beat everything below.

Prices

TTF front-month traded around €77.0/MWh on the last public read, down from €81 yesterday and about €7 below Monday’s €84 intraday high. Two sessions of drift lower, on the same Saudi-Hormuz-restart mechanic that took crude out, plus a Norwegian maintenance calendar that is starting to work in the other direction as the biggest single-day outages come off the schedule. This is the same TTF that gapped through the January 2023 peak four sessions ago; the fade has been orderly and the pattern of a hub with functioning liquidity has held.

NBP day-ahead sits at 195.79p/therm, down 11.71p over the last two sessions. The driver is the same TTF/crude backdrop plus a return of wind output across northwest Europe that dropped UK day-ahead power roughly 35% on the day. That is a big move and it is the mechanical inverse of what happened four sessions ago when the wind dropped: prompt power was up 142% in four sessions on a demand-into-thin-wind shove, and it is unwinding at roughly the same pace as the underlying wind resource returns. NBP’s year-on-year comparison is still around +150%, but the week-on-week is now firmly negative, which is what your Argus screen will show more precisely than these public trackers can.

The TTF/NBP divergence I flagged Monday has completely closed: TTF fell about 5% over two sessions, NBP about 5.7%, with the UK slightly ahead of the continent on the fade. The reason is exactly what the structural story predicted: NBP is a flow-not-stock market and it responds violently in both directions to marginal domestic supply, and wind returning is a bigger supply event than the gas-hub arb.

Storage

EU aggregate storage was ~68.3% full on 15 September per the AGSI+ series, still around 19 percentage points below the five-year seasonal norm. The country dispersion remains the story: Germany still ~55–56%, Italy ~84%, the same picture as Monday. That has not moved materially on today’s price action, which is the useful observation — a hub-price rise of ~30% peak-to-trough in a fortnight, followed by a fade, has not translated into a meaningful acceleration or deceleration in physical storage refill. Germany is still ~13 percentage points behind where it needs to be for a normal winter, and the mandatory storage target has been softened from 90% to 80% for the 2026 winter to reflect that reality.

UK storage is around 49% on the last public read, unchanged. Rough remains practically empty, the Centrica story stands, and the £2bn ask for regulatory-model inclusion still has no visible answer. Britain remains, functionally, going into this winter running on flow rather than stock, and today’s NBP fade is a reminder that flow-based markets are only cheap for as long as the marginal input remains cheap.

Supply

  • Qatar. QatarEnergy has extended force majeure on the Edison contract to end-September and the affected cargo count is now 24 (~3 bcm), of which Edison has replaced 17 (~1.6 bcm). Pakistani, Bangladeshi and other European buyers similarly notified through the same window. The satellite-imagery read is that heat signals are visible from three of Ras Laffan’s 14 LNG trains (units 1 and 2 at South, unit 5 at North) since early June, which is the closest thing to a partial restart signal available in the public data. The 12 undamaged trains restart on a two-month timeline conditional on strait security; the two damaged trains remain a 3–5 year rebuild. Nothing on the timing has moved this week.
  • Norway. The maintenance calendar has diverged this week between two publicly-available sources: MNI Markets has Ormen Lange fully offline 11 September to 2 October (24.5 mcm/d); other feeds have a different Ormen Lange schedule with a partial return around 16 September. I cannot reconcile these without your Argus/Gassco read. What is not disputed: Karsto processing plant maintenance ends today (17 September), returning 12.6 mcm/d. That is the biggest dated bullish restart of the week and it is in and priced already. Week 38 (starting Monday) is still the heaviest week of the calendar in aggregate, with the peak-outage day now the biggest live catalyst.
  • LNG into the UK. No update on sendout past the 10 September number. If your Kpler read has last-week receipts still around 8 mcm/d against a European demand that has been progressively fading with TTF, that is a routing-arb rather than a physical-tightness story; if higher, storage refill picks up.
  • Freight. Still no fresh public number on charter rates. Yesterday’s ask stands.

The policy layer

Nothing new in the last 24 hours. The IEA framing on strategic reserves remains the useful anchor for the winter (EU strategic reserves ~12 bcm in 2025, 3.5% of consumption), and the 80% mandatory storage-target relaxation for winter 2026 remains in force. The Commission’s next public statement on winter preparedness is the meaningful policy event still ahead, expected before the end of the month.

Where your data beats mine, ranked (1) Your Argus/Gassco read on today’s Norwegian export nominations — a two-source public divergence on Ormen Lange’s partial return date around 16–17 September and I cannot get a clean number from either. (2) Your Kpler read on European LNG receipts over the past week — the 8.1 mcm/d UK sendout number is now nine days old and either confirms tighter physical or reveals a routing arb toward the continent as TTF has faded. (3) Whether the German injection rate has picked up at all with today’s Karsto restart flowing into net-supply figures — 55.6% storage in mid-September clears winter only with sustained near-capacity injection through October. (4) Your desk’s current LNG charter rates. The March $161,750 print is now six months stale; the arb economics change materially if Atlantic rates have collapsed with US exports normalising.
