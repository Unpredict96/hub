01The five-minute version

Three data points landed on today’s calendar. All three came in hawkish. The market took the third one worst.

One. The Fed hiked to 3.75–4.00%, unanimously, and pencilled in one more.

Twelve votes to none for a 25bp move, the first hike since July 2023. The Summary of Economic Projections shows a median dot with one more 25bp hike this year, taking terminal to 4.00–4.25%. Sixteen of eighteen participants expect at least one further hike; four see two more. Chair Warsh has continued to abstain from submitting a dot since taking the role, which makes his presser tone the primary signal from the top of the committee — and today the tone was hawkish. “Inflation remains elevated,” and a pledge to seek a “timelier return” to 2%. The Dow, up through the decision, gave back 750 points into and after the press conference, closing −1.5%; S&P 500 −0.5%; Nasdaq −0.4%.

Two. UK CPI printed 3.1% in line — but the composition is the story.

Headline 3.1% y/y from 2.9%, monthly +0.5% (highest in four months). CPIH 3.3%. Core held at 2.6%. Services held at 3.4%. What moved was goods: goods CPI rose to 2.7% from 2.2%, with motor fuel inflation printing 23.0% year on year against 15.5% in July. Petrol +9.1p m/m to 161.3p/L; diesel +14.2p m/m to 181.8p/L. That is the crude rally reaching the CPI print with no filter left. The BoE meets in less than 20 hours and markets now price a 30% risk of a hike, from about 10–15% at Monday’s brief.

Three. The retail-sales upside surprise is what actually broke the tape.

US August retail sales +1.2% m/m against consensus +0.8%; ex-auto +1.4% against consensus +0.5%; a broad rise across categories. Consumer spending is not slowing into a $105 oil, a 5% ten-year, and a first Fed hike in three years. That is the print that makes “insurance move against energy shock” harder to sell and “the opening of a cycle” easier — and it landed at 08:30 New York, six hours before the FOMC.

02The Fed hiked. Warsh did the rest.

The decision was priced; the dots were middle-of-road; the press conference is what shifted the setup.

Twelve to zero. That number is the most under-remarked piece of the decision. A committee that split three ways in July on whether to prepare a hike delivered it unanimously seven weeks later — which is the strongest statement the Fed can make that this was not a discretionary move but one the data left them with. The three July hike-dissenters got the rest of the room; the two July hold-with-cut-language votes moved with them.

The SEP is more moderate than the tone. Median dot at 4.00–4.25% by year-end implies one more 25bp move across the remaining two meetings (October and December). Sixteen of eighteen participants expect at least one further hike; four expect two. If you priced 85–90% for today’s hike and considered whether the December decision would be another one, today gave you a majority in favour but not an overwhelming one — a market pricing 60–70% for December against 85–90% for today would fit the dots.

Kevin Warsh has, in three FOMCs, still not submitted a dot. That is not idiosyncrasy — it is design. A chair who does not commit to a projection preserves optionality on the messaging, and the messaging is where he differentiates himself from the Powell regime he replaced. Today’s press conference used the phrase “timelier return” to 2%, framed inflation as a persistence problem rather than a base-effect one, and volunteered that the committee is “prepared to do more.” Read those three phrases together and you get a chair who wants the market to price a higher probability of the December hike than the dots themselves imply.

The market gave him that. The Dow was up on the decision and gave back 750 points into and through the press conference, dragged by financials taking the yield-curve implications straight. S&P 500 −0.5%. Nasdaq only −0.4% — the megacaps that led Monday’s AI-pace rally cushioned the index-level damage; Alphabet closed higher on capex reassurances that dropped this morning, which is why the sub-composite move was less bad than the Dow implied.

The ten-year sits at 5.008%, essentially flat on the day. That is the most important read in the market. A ten-year that does not move on a Fed hike plus a hawkish presser is a bond market saying “you told us what we knew” — the 5.01% intraday print on 12 September had already discounted this decision. What the bond market has not yet discounted is a scenario where Warsh has to hike again in December and keep pushing into 2027. If tomorrow’s BoE goes 5–4 hold with hawkish language, or worse still surprises with a hike, that is the scenario the ten-year re-discounts, and it goes to 5.15–5.20% rather than back to 4.90%.

Retail sales was the print that made the whole day harder to argue against. +1.2% headline against +0.8% consensus is a beat; +1.4% ex-auto against +0.5% consensus is a landslide. Consumer spending accelerated in a month where petrol prices did the work in the CPI print — which means either (i) real consumer spending is genuinely running hotter than the labour market implies, or (ii) nominal spending is being pulled up by exactly the price pressure the Fed just hiked to contain. Both readings support the hike. Neither supports the market’s July view that this would be one-and-done insurance.

What this means for 78% of your bookThe core belief the fund sleeve is priced against is that this is the top of the hiking probability distribution. Warsh’s tone today shifted probability rightward — further hikes are more likely, and if delivered they land on a book whose average forward multiple has not yet compressed to reflect them. The lazy trade against that would be to reduce fund-level exposure. The problem, as Monday’s brief flagged, is that the lines where a trim is cheapest (ATT, GSK) do the least to the aggregate, and the lines where a trim is meaningful (VUAG General account, +£2,831 gain into CGT) are the most expensive to touch five weeks before a Budget with £13.8bn of headroom.

03UK CPI and the BoE setup

Composition matters more than the headline. The composition today is exactly what a hiking bank would want to see.

3.1% headline was in line and unremarkable. The rest of the print is not.

Motor fuel inflation is the immediate transmission of the crude rally. 23.0% y/y in August versus 15.5% in July, driven by petrol adding 9.1p per litre to 161.3p and diesel adding 14.2p to 181.8p. The month-on-month move of +0.5% is the largest in four months and is essentially fuel doing the work. If Brent sits at $100+ for the rest of September, next month’s print is worse before it’s better; the September CPI release on 21 October will land three days before the Budget, which is the political timing that makes this ugly for the Chancellor and not just for the MPC.

Core CPI at 2.6% held. Services at 3.4% held. That is the disinflation Bailey’s outgoing team can point to on the way out. It is also the reading the three MPC hawks — Pill, Greene and Mann — will say does not settle the case, because services at 3.4% is still well above target and goods inflation just moved up 50bp in a single print.

The vote count is what tomorrow is about. Consensus is 6–3 hold at 3.75%. The dissent has moved further hawkish at every meeting since March; a 5–4 hold with firmer inflation language, or a 4–5 majority for a hike, is now within the range of outcomes markets have to underwrite. Sonia futures now price roughly 30% risk of an actual hike tomorrow, up from 10–15% before this morning’s print.

Sterling is trading the sequence, not any one leg. GBP/USD closed at 1.3469, below $1.35 and the lowest since early August, on divergence between a Fed that hiked and a BoE that is still (probably) about to hold. If tomorrow’s BoE surprises hawkish, sterling closes the gap in a session. If it holds cleanly on a 6–3 with dovish language, cable can revisit 1.34. That is the range of outcomes the fund sleeve trades against because a stronger dollar mechanically inflates every USD line in GBP terms; the direct P&L effect on the book is a few tenths of a per cent in either direction, so it is a signal to watch rather than a position to size.

Where the book sits vs the BoE outcomes6–3 hold with dovish language: UKW rallies further, LifeStrategy UK sleeve steady, sterling weaker, USD fund lines gain a few tenths. 5–4 hold with hawkish language: UKW gives back today’s move, homebuilders sold again, gilts back through 5.45% on the 10y and toward 5.90% on the 30y. Surprise 4–5 hike: UKW takes a leg down worth 3–5%, sterling rallies through 1.35, and the fund sleeve prices a lower discount rate on UK exposure but also a genuine growth cost. That last outcome is not the base case; it is a real tail.

04The energy complex

Brent gave back the pipeline premium on hopes it comes back. It didn’t come back today, and the satellite imagery says weeks not days.

Brent settled at $105.83, down 2.7%. The proximate move was US officials briefing that the damaged Saudi East–West pipeline would restart within days. WTI followed to around $101. Both are still comfortably above where they sat a fortnight ago, and the physical picture is not what today’s tape implies.

Oil loadings at Yanbu remain suspended. Independent analysts working from satellite imagery of the pumping station argue for weeks of repair, not days. The Reuters framing was that the pipeline could take “several weeks” before nominal capacity is restored, and even then a partial restart at reduced flow. What the market appears to be trading is the political statement rather than the technical assessment — and the political statement has an incentive to be optimistic that the technical assessment does not.

The Hormuz situation is essentially unchanged from Monday. Crude transiting the strait runs near 7 mb/d versus roughly 20 mb/d pre-conflict; transits require Iranian permission; Tehran is reportedly still evaluating a service fee. That is a 65% reduction in flow through the world’s most concentrated crude chokepoint that has moved into being routine background rather than headline risk, which is itself the interesting fact.

The refined-product story is the one that reaches your CPI print. Roughly 800,000 b/d of diesel remains lost to the Russian export ban; roughly 1.2 mb/d of refined products lost through Hormuz disruption; combined conflicts have idled approximately 5 mb/d of refining capacity. Diesel cracks and gasoline cracks are what turn a $100 crude into a 23% motor fuel inflation print rather than a 12% one, and that is the transmission the ECB and BoE cannot look through even if they want to.

For your book, this is Shell. London closed at 3,652.50p going into the American afternoon; Brent then settled −2.7%, which under normal energy-equity beta gets you Shell down roughly 1.5–2% on the London open tomorrow. The five-week rally that took Shell from around 3,050p to 3,652p has been an integrated-major bid on the crude curve rather than a Q3 earnings anticipation, and the crude curve gave a signal today. It is not the end of the trade, but it is the first session where the sequencing is not one-way.

Your Kpler read on European LNG receipts this week is the one that would confirm or refute that in a way I cannot.

Storage

EU aggregate was 66.59% on 6 September per AGSI+, versus a five-year average north of 80% and against 82% at this point last year — one of the lowest pre-winter positions in two decades. On 13 September the country dispersion still tells the story: Italy 84.4% and Germany 55.6%. Germany at 55.6% going into a winter with Qatar still under force majeure is the number that dictates whether TTF caps at €90 in a normal winter or breaks €120 in a cold one.

UK storage is around 49% full on the last public read; Rough remains, on Centrica’s account, practically empty because the forward curve does not pay for injection, and Centrica’s August statement was that Rough will close by spring 2027 absent inclusion in the regulatory support model. That is the same picture as Monday, and the fact that nothing has changed structurally about UK storage in five sessions of NBP prints above 200p is the point — the constraint is capital allocation, not capacity, and capital allocation is what the £2bn ask is about.
