02Muse, CPUs, and AMD at a trillion

The rally you got today was a re-pricing of a piece of the AI stack the market had been ignoring.

The catalyst is architecture, not a headline. Meta’s Muse launched twelve days ago and pushed to No. 1 on the US free App Store within ten. That in itself would not have moved AMD 9% in a single session; the shift is in what a Muse-shaped agent implies for the physical stack under it. Each active Muse runs on a dedicated secure cloud virtual machine that owns its own browser, CPU, memory and storage. The agent persists after the app closes — that means the VM is up whether the user is present or not. Model reasoning still calls the GPU. Everything else — tool use, code execution, browser navigation, orchestration between subtasks — is CPU work. Sell-side calc through the weekend rescaled inference-era CPU-to-GPU ratios upward by multiples once you introduce a per-agent VM footprint.

The tape confirmed it. AMD +8.9% to $609.38 with a $615.99 intraday high, both all-time. Market cap peaked in the session at approximately $1.005trn on the print before easing back to just under $995bn at the close — the first time AMD has touched the trillion mark on any intraday tick. Intel gained 12–14% and finished the top S&P 500 performer, with a related sub-story from AUO packaging that added to the flow. Arm Holdings, which is where hyperscaler custom-silicon is concentrated (Google Axion N4A, Microsoft Cobalt 200, Amazon Graviton) climbed 13–17%. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index rose 3.9%, its fifth consecutive up-session; five in a row is a rare enough sequence in the index that it functions as its own signal about the direction of institutional flow.

What this changes about the AI-capex debate is the shape of the demand curve, not the height. Since spring the argument on this desk has been that CPU workloads inside agent-heavy inference would come to matter, but the market was still pricing GPU as the whole cake. Today it stopped. The read across the book is asymmetric — not because Nvidia loses (it did not; NVDA finished the tape roughly flat-to-slightly-up on the day, and its pricing power stays intact for now) but because the merchant CPU vendors just captured a piece of AI narrative that had accrued to GPU-only names. Your direct AMD is 1.6175 shares, worth roughly £457 on the day’s close — a £40 addition on the position. The look-through matters more: AMD sits inside ATT at about 2.6% of NAV per the last public factsheet, inside VanEck Semiconductor at ~9% and inside the Vanguard S&P/Global funds at fractional weights that add to real money when you look at the sleeve gross.

The specific mechanic to watch is not Muse itself — consumer AI apps come and go — but whether the pattern repeats. If the next agent launch from any of Google, OpenAI, Anthropic or Apple follows a comparable per-instance VM architecture, the CPU-repricing tape reruns without needing a fresh catalyst. Muse is the first Class-A datapoint for the thesis; the second one lands the trade properly. That is the calendar you should be reading for the next four weeks. First likely catalyst: OpenAI DevDay (the exact date has not been finalised publicly — ask your Argus / SP Global calendar what they have) and Google’s next Gemini agent update. If either of those puts a comparable architecture into general availability, the repricing accelerates.

One skeptic’s noteMuse’s No. 1 App Store ranking is a consumer engagement signal; it does not automatically translate to sustained inference volume. Meta earns nothing directly from it and has not disclosed active-agent counts. Assume half the sell-side CPU-uplift math is order-of-magnitude fair and half is enthusiasm — the direction is right, the multiplier isn’t yet. That is why the trade you own indirectly (through funds) works better than a trade you would have to size directly.

03Trump–Xi Thursday: the AI-China trade goes to dinner

The largest concentration of US AI-market-cap CEOs at a single Washington event on record.

Xi Jinping arrives Wednesday. The state dinner is Thursday evening. Confirmed guests at the top table: Elon Musk (SpaceX), Jeff Bezos (Amazon), Jensen Huang (Nvidia), Sam Altman (OpenAI), Sundar Pichai (Alphabet), Michael Dell (Dell Technologies), Jane Fraser (Citi), Tim Cook (Apple), and David Sacks as former AI czar. Bilateral agenda includes AI safety, export controls (specifically the H20 licensing framework), rare-earth flows and trade. Semafor and the Washington Times both had the guest list on Friday. The list is the point — nine of the ten largest US AI-dependent equities are represented in one room, in the presence of both heads of state, three days before Nvidia’s next fiscal quarter effectively locks in.

What is actually at stake divides into two live threads. First, H20 licensing: this is the version of Nvidia’s data-centre GPU that was engineered to sit inside export controls, and the tape has spent six weeks trying to price whether volume flows resume. Any softening of the language coming out of Thursday would send Nvidia and the Chinese hyperscaler-adjacent trade higher on the same session; any hardening would sit on the same names into month-end. Second, rare earths: your Fidelity European fund has ~10% capital-goods exposure that reads through to industrial input costs, and China’s licensing regime on rare-earth exports has been the marginal input-cost story for European autos and defence since spring.

How the market gets to Thursday from here. The path is straightforward: Tuesday and Wednesday will trade the anticipation, with the semi complex likely staying bid on the same Muse mechanic and the AI-China basket (which you touch through DiDi, EMQQ, and the Chinese exposure inside Fundsmith and Global All Cap) catching a sympathy bid. The overhang on any of these positions is not the meeting itself; it is the risk of a headline in the seventy-two hours around it that reprices the export-control regime.

Bear read at full strength. The meeting is optics; the meaningful decisions on H20 licensing and rare-earth flows sit inside the Commerce Department and the National Security Council and take months to work through. The market has repeatedly rewarded photo-op summits with three-session rallies and then re-sold them into month-end when nothing changed. Do not price a Thursday photo as a policy shift. Do price it as a volatility event.

For the book directly. DiDi, EMQQ, and the Chinese lines inside global funds together are ~£1,850 of direct exposure and perhaps another £2,000–3,000 of look-through. Move the whole China basket 5% on a positive Thursday reaction and the book adds maybe £200. Move it 5% down and the same magnitude works against you. This is not a size worth positioning around; it is a size worth being aware of when you read Friday morning headlines.

04Oil, Hormuz, and a slower Fed conversation

Brent through $102 with Riyadh under air-raid alert. The market is trading fundamentals now, not geopolitics.

Brent settled around $101.6, off ~2.6% on the day, with WTI briefly under $100 in intraday trading. That is the third consecutive down session; the peak-to-trough drawdown from last Monday’s intraday high is now approximately 7–8%. The catalyst layer today: Saudi Arabia issued air-raid warnings for Riyadh — the first since the March/April peak of the US–Iran war — plus alerts for Red Sea hubs including Yanbu; a projectile struck a tanker as it entered the Strait of Hormuz; US embassies re-issued regional security alerts; Treasury Secretary Bessent confirmed all Iranian airlines will be shut down worldwide on Wednesday 23 September. The crude curve did not care. That is genuinely the story.

The re-routing infrastructure Saudi Arabia established through the summer — roughly 2.8mn b/d moving through Hormuz over the last week per satellite tracking — is now the operating baseline. Iranian permission still gates the flow; the fee structure appears settled. Meanwhile refined-product supply-chain damage from the pipeline attacks and the Ras Laffan strikes continues, and the ~5 mb/d idle refinery estimate has not changed. So the shape of the exposure has flipped: the physical shortage in crude is priced out; the physical constraint on distillate is still live and continues to run into UK and European pump prices on a two-week lag.

Rates and the Fed sit against this backdrop. The US 10-year is holding around the 5.00% level it re-took Friday, with the 2-year firm near 4.72%. Goolsbee spoke at OMFIF this morning — the Chicago Fed president is generally middle-of-the-road on the committee, and his remarks were consistent with the current pricing structure: markets have moved to roughly 50/50 for an October hike and now fully price a December move, with another priced by June 2027. That is a meaningfully hawkish shift from where December sat entering Wednesday’s Warsh press conference, and it stands even on a day when oil fell 2%. The read is that the market has stopped needing crude to push the Fed — the labour-market and services-inflation data are now doing that work.

UK gilts held. The long end continues to price the BoE’s QT rewrite. Ten-year yield remains ~10–15bp below the pre-decision Wednesday close; the 30-year is holding onto Thursday’s ~12bp rally. Sterling drifted — GBP/USD closed London around 1.3382, marginally below Friday’s 1.3395 — and that FX drift is the mechanism converting today’s London-red into a portfolio that still books a small positive tomorrow morning.

Where this leaves the sequenceDecember Fed pricing has crept to 70–75% on today’s speak-and-tape combination, up from ~65% on Friday. The next data point large enough to reset that is US September NFP on 3 October, then September CPI on 15 October, both ahead of the Nvidia print. Between here and there, the calendar is largely quiet on scheduled macro — the Xi state dinner on Thursday and the Norwegian outage peak on Tuesday 23rd are the two dated events with the highest chance of setting a session-defining tone.
