02Nvidia: the CEO is back, the questions are not answered

Monday priced removing the governance discount. Tuesday started to price the fact that the accounting question and the export-control question are still open, and the second one got worse.

What happened

On Sunday night Nvidia’s board voted unanimously to reinstate Jensen Huang as president, CEO and board director, effective immediately. Harvey Jones remains chair. The release says the independent committee’s investigation (independent directors, with Joon H. Kim and Wachtell Lipton) “will proceed to conclusion,” and the board has appointed a new chief compliance officer. On 17 November the board had said it did not intend to reinstate him. What changed in six days was public pressure from the President, who called for the reinstatement repeatedly and has since claimed credit (NPR), and support from other tech CEOs. On Tuesday Forbes, citing the WSJ, reported that Huang disclosed additional extramarital relationships to the board on Sunday, before the vote. That suggests the board went into the vote knowing more than had been published, and voted for him anyway.

Why Monday doesn’t settle Friday’s question

On Friday the market was asking whether Nvidia’s reported numbers can be trusted. Reinstating the CEO doesn’t answer that. It is the same management, now backed by a board that last week said it would not take him back. Three things are where they were on Friday:

The bull case for the AI chain has improved: the founder is back, Q3 numbers were strong, and the S&P is no longer in a correction. The bear case is the same as it was on Friday, plus the White House now visibly influencing a board decision, which is a governance point in its own right. Goldman’s “not a clearing event” summed up what the tape did today.

The argument for pausing, in full

Since Monday’s settle, three things support Brent. The Houthis hit a tower in Riyadh (11 reported injured), and Saudi Arabia’s finance minister said the kingdom is prepared for a long conflict. Iran says three tankers caught fire after hitting mines in Hormuz, and Iran’s army chief has ordered the strait to stay closed until seven conditions are met. A third US carrier has joined the Gulf. Kpler data (as reported by Trading Economics; I have no Kpler access) shows Hormuz crude flows averaged about 9.5 mb/d in the ten days to 19 November, against 12.2 mb/d in October. January Brent was back around $90.8 tonight. One report (DSIJ) says Goldman raised its December Brent forecast to $105. I could not confirm that, and Trading Economics describes Goldman as warning of a 2027 glut, so I give it no weight. On this reading Monday was a one-day dip on a risk-on rotation. If you trim, you are selling on a settle that has already been retraced, in a taxable account.

Why I don’t think that is strong enough:

  • The front-month is about to fall mechanically. By ICE’s expiry rule, January Brent stops trading on Monday 30 November. From 1 December the front-month is February, which settled tonight at $88.78, about $2 below January. Unless February rallies $1.22 or more by Monday, the rule will read below $90 again on the first settle in December, without any change in the market. If you wait for a second signal, the roll will give you one.
  • The bearish drivers are structural, and the bullish ones are headlines. The IEA says Gulf crude exports are back to 74% of pre-war levels and the market goes into surplus this quarter. Brent is down about 22% on the month. OPEC+ meets on Sunday to set 2027 policy, and Iraq has already rejected the group’s assessment of its capacity, which is how quota discipline starts to break. Petrobras plans on $74 Brent in 2027. Three weeks of headlines like tonight’s have each lifted the price for a session and then faded.
  • The rule exists so you don’t have to make this call. On Friday the rule kept you from selling at $90.06 intraday, and that was right. Monday is the mirror image. A rule you honour only when it suits you isn’t doing anything.
  • Shell isn’t pricing Brent anyway. The shares are −0.6% over a two-day period in which January Brent fell 3.5% and recovered about 1.6%. The trim is about concentration in a +153% line in II General, at a size that probably fits inside the allowance. Whether it is “right” on oil won’t be known for months.

What would make me wrong: a real Hormuz closure, meaning tanker traffic and war-risk premia actually changing, not an order from the army chief, would take Brent back over $100. In that case the 75% you keep carries the upside, which is what the 25% sizing was for. If February settles above $92 into the roll, with leg 3 still soft, that would be the point to discuss whether the rule should be re-armed before the next 25% tranche. That is a later conversation, not tonight’s.

Prices

Kpler’s estimate of about 12 mtpa of Qatari capacity offline through 2027 (as reported by Newsquawk) is the physical anchor that crude doesn’t have. A year ago TTF was about €30.

Storage

EU aggregate: 69.17% on 22 November (about 781 TWh), with 2,868 GWh/d withdrawn against 714 GWh/d injected that day. A net draw of about 2.15 TWh/d is about 0.19 points a day, double the ~0.09/day of the mild week before. Trading Economics has it under 69% on the 24th. A year ago it was 81.2%, and the five-year average is about 89%. The gap to the average is about 20 points and still widening. Forecasts call for this week to run 3–5°C below normal in north-west Europe and up to 8°C below normal in early December. That would push draws towards 0.3+ points a day, putting the EU near 60% by mid-December. Germany: no figure newer than about 57% (AGSI, 17 November). INES’s warning (lower fill levels mean lower maximum withdrawal rates, and a shortfall of up to 6.6 TWh a month in an extreme cold scenario) becomes relevant earlier if the early-December cold arrives. Italy about 91% and UK about 40% with Rough at 0.0 TWh are both last week’s numbers and not re-verified. Rough still matters structurally: the UK has no seasonal storage to draw on this week, so the cold has to be met from flows and sendout, which is consistent with the NBP premium holding.
