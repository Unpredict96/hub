02Iran, Hormuz and oil through $100

A specific diplomatic offer — not a rumour — met a sanctions cliff arriving in 24 hours.

The mechanic behind today’s move is Qatari mediation. Doha has been passing proposals in both directions since roughly the first week of September, and today one of them was reported specifically: Iran indicated it could reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days if the United States eases military pressure. Trump publicly signalled openness to meeting Iranian leadership, giving the language the second endorsement it needed. The parallel signal — Iran’s parliament submitting a bill to withdraw from nuclear treaties — is the kind of contradictory noise a live diplomatic channel produces when both sides are trying to keep leverage. The tape ignored the parliament bill and priced the mediation.

Brent traded down to ~$98/bbl, WTI briefly ~$89, the fourth consecutive down session, and the peak-to-trough drawdown from last Monday’s ~$107 intraday high is now roughly 10%. The dispersion across feeds is real: one settle read has Brent at $100.34, another has it at $98, and a Yahoo intraday screen has $98.6 with WTI $89. That dispersion is why the story is not the print but the direction. The direction is unambiguous. The two-week thesis on the desk was that Saudi re-routing had made the Hormuz risk premium hollow, and today the market took the next step: if Iran itself moves off the tanker-attack posture, there is no premium left to unwind because the tape has already unwound it.

The counter-cliff arrives Wednesday. Bessent’s all-Iranian-airlines shutdown takes effect on 23 September — the sanction is on the fuel, landing services, ticketing infrastructure and dollar-clearing chain for anyone servicing an Iranian carrier globally, not on the airlines themselves. That is a genuine escalation aimed at the operational plumbing rather than the flag. The read Thursday morning is whether Iran responds to a landed sanction with a tanker incident (which would price the risk premium back in one session) or with a diplomatic acceptance (which would be the exit ticket on any remaining premium). Neither outcome is base case; both are live.

For the FTSE oil complex the tape was oddly forgiving. Shell finished around 3,510p, up 0.6% on the day per the late London prints I could get, and BP was quoted down 1–2% on the same crude tick — the divergence is the LNG exposure (Shell has it, BP doesn’t) and Shell’s Q3 dividend catalyst 29 October, which the market is refusing to discount ahead of the confirmation. Note that headline dispersion here: one FTSE 100 recap has Shell down 1% and BP down 2.4%, another has Shell up 0.6%. The published London close on your II General account tomorrow morning is the number that matters; treat 3,510p as the working figure.

The Hormuz math for the deskIf Iran genuinely permits transit at pre-conflict volumes within seven days, Saudi Arabia’s ~2.8mn b/d Hormuz throughput returns to a ~7mn b/d baseline within a month, distillate re-supply resumes, and the ~5 mb/d idle refinery estimate begins to compress. The direct read across to gas is via distillate-to-fuel-oil substitution in Asian power — less pricing pressure on marginal LNG demand into Japan and Korea, which reads back to a softer TTF. The base case remains that the diplomatic offer is real but stops short of full transit; the tail case that matters is the reverse — a tanker incident tomorrow that reprices the whole thing in six hours.

03Hawkish Fed drumbeat, UK borrowing shock

Two Fed hawks paid to make the case; ONS handed the Chancellor the wrong Budget backdrop.

The Fed communications day featured two speakers doing the substantive lifting. St. Louis’s Alberto Musalem said gradual tightening applied early is preferable, and that policy is still accommodative — a straightforward hawk making a straightforward hawkish case, which the market did not need but priced anyway. Richmond’s Tom Barkin was more useful: he acknowledged the argument that inflation would return to target on its own without additional Fed help but explicitly said “supply shocks aren’t proving short-lived,” which is the sentence that keeps the door open on a December hike. Goolsbee’s Peterson event added a coda from yesterday morning’s OMFIF remarks about supply shocks plus AI-linked investment keeping inflation stubborn.

The pricing update: December Fed pricing sits ~70–75% for a hike, essentially unchanged from Monday’s close. The 10-year yield was ~4.95%, down about 1bp on the day. The 2-year was little changed near 4.72%. The macro fact that matters for you is not the yield path itself but its second derivative — the dollar. DXY held above 100; GBP/USD at 1.3357, the weakest since late July. That is the mechanism converting a slightly-down US equity day into a book that closes flat-to-slightly-up in sterling.

The UK story is fiscal, not monetary. ONS published August public sector net borrowing at £18.3bn, the second-highest August on record (behind 2020), £3.5bn above the OBR forecast for the month, and running the fiscal-year-to-date £8.1bn above OBR. That is a genuine forecast miss and it does not give the Chancellor room to rewrite the numbers in October in a way that supports a growth-oriented autumn Budget. Two things follow. First, sterling has just found a domestic reason to weaken independent of the Fed — and the FX picking up the fiscal tab is exactly the arithmetic that flatters your dollar look-through today. Second, the QT rewrite that gave the Chancellor slightly more long-end room has just had part of that room absorbed by an accelerating deficit run-rate, which puts your UKW discount-rate thesis on a slightly softer footing for the Autumn Budget on 28 October, even before you know what the actual Budget contains.

The read across your book from the fiscal update is small and steady. The mechanical FX effect is worth ~£200 tonight on the day’s ~0.2% sterling giveback. The strategic effect is that the Budget window has narrowed — the next data point that would either force the Chancellor towards more borrowing or free her from it is Friday’s Q2 GDP third estimate. Between now and 28 October, the tape’s bias is towards weaker sterling on any softer UK print and towards flat-to-slightly-weaker on the long end if the market re-prices the Budget as more expensive.

Where this leaves your dollar lengthOn any reading, half your book is dollar-denominated on look-through and none of it is hedged. Cumulative GBP/USD move since 11 September is now approximately −1.2%, worth roughly £1,100 in reported value on the dollar sleeve. That is a real number that is bigger than any single-line direct move you have booked in the same window. It also reverses if the Fed dovish-pivots — and December Fed pricing at 70–75% is a level where a soft NFP on 3 October or a soft CPI on 15 October could take a piece out of the FX tailwind quickly. The tail I keep flagging is unhedged US exposure at Nasdaq record highs — the compounding risk is that if the AI trade rolls at the same time as sterling strengthens, you get hit both sides. Nothing to trade on that tonight; the exposure to size against is the whole book, not one line.
