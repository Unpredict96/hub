What changed physically

The decision, restated

On Tuesday I argued against pausing for four reasons. One of them is now wrong, and you should know which.

What no longer holds: the roll. I said February, at $88.78, would read below $90 on the first December settle, so waiting would get you a second signal mechanically. February has since settled at $90.64. If it holds above $90 through Monday’s January expiry, the rule reads clean on 1 December. The mechanical second signal is gone.

What has got stronger for pausing: the outage is physical. Tuesday’s brief said that “a real Hormuz closure, meaning tanker traffic and war-risk premia actually changing” would take Brent back over $100. A halted Saudi export terminal is close to that, even if it isn’t Hormuz itself. If loadings stay down into December, $100 is plausible.

What still holds: the rule wasn’t really an oil call, and the size covers the upside. Over Monday to Wednesday Shell moved 0.2%, 0.4% and 0.1% while January Brent swung $3.20, $2.03 and $3.20. It only moved with crude on Thursday, +1.91%, when London was the only major market open. The trim is about £1.8k out of a 158%-up line in a taxable account, sized to fit inside the CGT allowance. If Brent goes to $100, the 146 shares you keep carry it, which is the reason for 25% rather than 50%.

What has got stronger for doing it: the price. You’d sell at 3,623.5p rather than 3,552p.

There are two honest readings, and the choice between them is yours, not mine. Reading A, the rule lapsed: it said “next session”, Tuesday’s settle was already back above $90, so the window closed with the condition false. Under that reading you hold 195 shares and the rule re-arms. Reading B, the rule fired and the execution was late: a missed execution window doesn’t cancel a signal you set in calmer conditions, so you do the trim at the next open. My view is that Reading B is more consistent with why you wrote the rule, and the price works in your favour. Reading A is a legitimate rule design, as long as you make the choice explicitly and write it down. What I’d avoid is drifting: not trimming, not saying so, and letting the rule lose its meaning. Whichever you choose, log it in holdings_changes.md.

What would make the trim wrong: Ras Tanura stays shut into the OPEC+ meeting and Brent settles above $100, with Shell finally pricing the barrel. You’d have sold a quarter at the bottom of a move. What would make holding wrong: loadings restart within days, OPEC+ shows a quota fight, and February goes back below $90 on the roll. That repeats Monday, and you’d still be 195 shares long in a +158% line in II General.

Inflation data on both sides of the Atlantic says hike. On Wednesday the bond market bought duration anyway, which tells you what it is more worried about.

Initial claims 189k (200k expected), continuing claims 1.697m. New home sales 701k (650k expected). That is an economy running hot with sticky core inflation. Yet Treasuries bull-flattened: the 2-year at 4.639% (−4.8bp), the 10-year at 4.852% (−7.6bp) and the 30-year at 5.243% (−7.5bp). Trading Economics puts the 10-year intraday low near 4.81%, the lowest since 8 September. The $44bn 7-year auction tailed by 1bp, the biggest since May 2024, and the rally still held. December hike pricing slipped to 24.4bp (from 25.7bp), still fully priced. Waller reportedly opened the door to 50bp, but said he could support 25bp with forward guidance. March slipped to 54.5bp. The FOMC blackout starts this weekend. CPI on 10 December lands the day after the 9 December decision, so the Fed votes without it.

My reading is that the rally priced growth risk from tariffs and the AI-capex wobble, not inflation. The 301 proposal is a negative supply shock with a lag, and the market is front-running the slowdown rather than the price effect. It also makes Wednesday the first session in this drawdown where bonds hedged equities. Through October and most of November, stocks and bonds fell together. For a fund-heavy book that is a better correlation regime, not a worse one.

UK. The Ofgem cap is the event. January–March: £2,166 for a typical dual-fuel household, +26% (+£443). Ofgem’s table puts the electricity unit rate at 10.96p/kWh, from 7.97p, partly offset by a £100 payment and an electricity VAT cut. Using roughly 3.5–4% CPI weight for household energy, I get about +0.9 to +1.0pp on January CPI. That is my arithmetic, not the BoE’s. With CPI at 3.6%, a 4.5%+ print in the February release is plausible. The Bank’s own decision page says inflation rises to about 5% by mid-2027. The MPC divided along familiar lines:

  • Bailey: the peak would be lower if current energy prices held. Sterling wholesale gas is off its late-October highs, so this is a soft dovish lean.
  • Lombardelli: the likely peak is below the November forecast. Same direction.
  • Mann: raise rates further and sooner. She is the hawk, and one report says she voted 50bp in November (unverified).
  • Greene: rates may need to rise faster if the Middle East conflict drags on.

Pricing for a 17 December hike to 4.25% is about 80% (Bloomberg via Tradingpedia, Thursday), slightly below the 82% after Greene on Tuesday. Trading Economics says Tuesday’s falling oil had trimmed hike expectations, and Wednesday’s rebound partly reversed that. The 10-year gilt was about 5.16% on Thursday (+2bp), and the 2-year has been trading either side of 5%. Separately, the BoE proposed cutting the capital requirement for the largest lenders by 60bp, and Breeden flagged gilt repo resilience. Neither moves the book. GBP/USD was 1.3115, 1.3116 and 1.3107 over Tuesday to Thursday, off Monday’s 1.3010 low and steady.

Euro area. The October ECB minutes said a “large number” of members could have supported a hike at that meeting, which is more hawkish than the decision itself. The Governing Council recommended Hernández de Cos to succeed Lagarde on 7 February. A decision on raising banks’ minimum reserves was pushed to at least December. European equities fell hard on Wednesday (Stoxx 50 −1.88%, IBEX −2.0%, FTSE MIB −1.81%), with tariffs and tech, then bounced on Thursday (Stoxx 50 +2.11% to 6,019, DAX +2.14%). What it means for your book. For UKW, the MPC on 17 December is still the event, and the cap makes a hike more likely, not less. But a 26% cap rise comes from wholesale prices that also feed UKW’s merchant revenues and NAV, so it is not a clean negative. The shares moved −1.0% on Wednesday and +1.8% on Thursday, tracking risk appetite more than gilts. For the funds, Wednesday’s bond rally was the first time this drawdown that duration cushioned equities. Nothing to do.
