01The five-minute version

Three things earned space today: US August core PCE printed 3.0% YoY and 0.2% MoM, three tenths below consensus on both measures, but the BEA rolled out a five‑year retroactive methodology overhaul in the same release; ADP came in at +90k against +68k consensus and Chicago PMI hit 58.8 against a 51.2 consensus, a 12‑point beat; and the UK Q2 GDP third estimate was revised up to 0.5% QoQ with business investment growth lifted to 5.2% YoY from a 0.8% initial reading. The 10‑year eased two basis points to 5.23%. October hike odds have gone from 90%+ a week ago to about 44%. December still prints just under 90%. That is the shape of the day.

One. Soft PCE with a methodology asterisk — October is off, December still is not.

Fed’s preferred inflation gauge: headline PCE +0.3% MoM / +3.4% YoY against a Reuters consensus of +0.3% / +3.7%. Core PCE +0.2% MoM / +3.0% YoY against +0.3% / +3.3%. Both measures printed three tenths under expectations on the year‑on‑year read. The 3.0% core YoY is unchanged from a downwardly‑revised July reading, itself a step down from the June 3.1% carried across the summer. Taken at face value, this was the softest inflation print of the third quarter.

Take it at face value carefully. Buried in the release, the Bureau of Economic Analysis rolled out a comprehensive methodology overhaul of how it measures spending on software, investment management and legal services, and the revised methods were applied retroactively to data from the past five years. That is not a footnote. The BEA’s summary language framed the change as bringing consumer‑services measurement in line with the CPI treatment, but the practical consequence is that the three‑tenths surprise this month is measurably smaller than it looks — some fraction of it is definitional. The BLS is not affected; PPI is not affected; CPI came in at 2.9% for August three weeks ago and the divergence between the two indices has just widened to 40bp. The market took the print at face value first and will start disaggregating it inside 48 hours.

The reaction: 10‑year eased 1–2bp to 5.23%. That is essentially no move, and it is the single most instructive data point on the tape. The consumer‑confidence 12‑year low on Tuesday plus a soft PCE this morning is exactly the combination that a fortnight ago would have priced a growth‑scare bond rally. It printed instead as a modest dovish repricing in the October window — the CME’s October hike odds fell from around 70% pre‑print (already down from 90%+ a fortnight ago on Williams’ Tuesday speech) to about 44%. December odds remain just under 90%. The tape has decided that PCE at 3.0% and the labour data holding still add up to one more hike, and the only question is October vs December. The Fed decision framework has bought the message.

Two. ADP +90k and Chicago PMI 58.8 — the growth‑is‑fine channel opened wide.

Two hot prints landed on the same session as the soft PCE and neutralised each other in the bond tape. ADP private payrolls +90k in September, against a Reuters consensus of +68k, and against a prior read of +36k (revised from an even softer initial print). Nela Richardson’s ADP commentary called it “a rebound after three months of decline.” Base pay for private‑sector workers rose 3.2% YoY, gross pay 4.7%. It is not a barn‑burner in isolation, but +90k against +68k consensus and a bottoming pattern is the labour‑market signal that keeps the December hike alive.

The Chicago PMI came in at 58.8 against a 51.2 consensus and a 47.1 prior. That is a 7.6‑point beat and an 11.7‑point sequential jump. Chicago has been volatile all cycle, but a print above 55 with the prior below 50 usually says that regional manufacturing has turned. Coming a week after the S&P Global flash composite PMI hit 58.4, this is two separate manufacturing/services signals telling you private‑sector expansion is running hot. The input‑prices sub‑index is what the desk will pull tomorrow — last month’s S&P flash had it at 66.4, the highest since October 2022. That is the number that keeps the Powell inner circle uncomfortable.

The net: Dow −0.86% to 50,908.79 on the growth‑is‑fine channel dominating for cyclicals, S&P 500 +0.20% to roughly 7,686 on the dovish‑PCE channel dominating for tech, and the Nasdaq Composite up around 0.9% intraday before settling into a solid Q3 close. The Dow will snap its five‑month winning streak on the September number. Q3 net for the S&P was around +2%; Nasdaq roughly the same. Not a bad quarter into a stagflation‑pricing regime.

Three. UK Q2 GDP revised up to 0.5% — and sterling didn’t pay.

07:00 BST: ONS final Q2 GDP came in at +0.5% QoQ, an upward revision from the +0.4% third estimate (and the +0.3% second estimate before that). The +2.0% YoY reading was revised up from +1.9%. The composition matters: services output growth 0.6% was the primary driver, and business investment was sharply revised up to +5.2% YoY from a +0.8% initial estimate — a 4.4pp upward revision on the single component the OBR forecast leans on hardest. First‑half growth combined (Q1 +0.6% + Q2 +0.5%) puts the UK at the top of the G7 for H1 2026, ahead of the US on this specific comparison.

Sterling’s response was subdued. GBP/USD closed 1.3224, essentially flat on Tuesday’s 1.3230 and inside a narrow 1.3202–1.3274 range. The pound touched 1.3260s intraday before giving it back on the ADP print. The message is that FX is not trading UK growth; it is trading the fiscal signal into the 28 October Budget. A stronger H1 gives Reeves marginally more headroom on the fiscal arithmetic, but the August borrowing overshoot (£3.5bn above OBR path) is what set the market’s tone and a single quarter of revisions doesn’t offset it. Nine sessions before the Budget the market is trading the deficit, not the numerator. That said, the upward revision is real and load‑bearing for the Budget arithmetic — it just doesn’t show up in the cable print yet.

02PCE cooled on paper, the BEA rewrote the paper, and the belly still won’t bulge

The three‑tenths surprise across headline and core was real; the methodology overhaul that landed with it is what stopped the 10‑year rallying. October is off the table; December still isn’t.

The data. BEA released the August PCE report at 12:30 BST. Headline PCE +0.3% MoM / +3.4% YoY, against Reuters consensus of +0.3% / +3.7%. Core PCE +0.2% MoM / +3.0% YoY, against +0.3% / +3.3%. Both YoY measures came in three tenths under expectations. The 3.4% headline was unchanged from a downwardly‑revised July print; the 3.0% core was also unchanged from a downwardly‑revised July. The seasonally adjusted headline MoM at 0.3% was in line, so the surprise sits mostly in the year‑on‑year math on the base effect. Personal consumption for August was 0.6%, in line with expectations. Personal income was 0.4%. This is a tape that says: household spending is still growing, incomes are still growing, inflation is coming in a fraction below where the Street had it.

The methodology asterisk. The BEA release was accompanied by an announcement that it has completed a five‑year retroactive overhaul of how it measures spending on software, investment management and legal services, with the revised methodology applied to data from 2021 through the current release. The BEA framed the change as bringing consumer‑services measurement into line with equivalent CPI treatments. The practical implication is that some fraction of today’s three‑tenths surprise is definitional rather than economic. Your Argus/Bloomberg terminal will have the release notes by the desk open Thursday; the specific line to watch is PCE services less housing YoY, which is where the software/investment‑management overhaul lands most heavily. CPI for August three weeks ago printed 2.9% YoY; the PCE-CPI gap has now widened to 40bp, wider than any month across the last two years. That widening is the tell that the methodology change has done a lot of the work.

The rate‑path reaction. Fed funds futures repriced the near path but held the medium path. October hike odds fell from about 70% pre‑print (already down from 90%+ two weeks ago) to roughly 44% by close. This is the third leg of the Williams speech dominating the tape — Tuesday’s Buffalo remarks (“no urgency”) plus today’s soft print is the two‑sided message the market needed to price October off. December hike odds remain just under 90%, essentially unchanged from Tuesday. That is a message: the Fed is signalling patience, but the tape is telling you the terminal doesn’t move — whichever meeting hosts it, one more 25bp is coming. 10Y +/−1–2bp to 5.23%. A three‑point PCE surprise usually moves the belly 4–6bp; today’s move was well inside the noise. That is the market telling you it is partially discounting the print because of the methodology issue.

ADP and Chicago PMI printed the counter. ADP September +90k against +68k Reuters consensus, +36k prior. The +36k prior was revised from an even softer initial. Base pay +3.2% YoY, gross pay +4.7%. Nela Richardson’s commentary: this is a rebound after three months of decline in ADP’s series, and the labour market is not deteriorating in the way the consumer confidence 12‑year low suggested Tuesday. Chicago PMI 58.8 against 51.2 consensus, 47.1 prior. A 7.6‑point beat and an 11.7‑point sequential jump. Combined with last week’s S&P Global flash composite PMI 58.4, this is two separate manufacturing/services surveys pointing to genuinely hot Q3‑end activity. The Fed’s dual‑mandate calculation gets worse, not better, on this combination: inflation cooling a fraction but activity accelerating with the input‑prices sub‑index still elevated is exactly the setup that gives Williams cover to reaffirm the December hike.

The tape’s split reaction confirms. Nasdaq Composite up ~0.9% intraday on the dovish‑PCE channel that fuels tech; Dow −0.86% to 50,908.79 on the growth‑is‑fine channel that leans cyclicals into higher rates for longer; S&P 500 net around +0.20% to 7,686. Dow snapping its five‑month winning streak on the September number is the specific data point that says leadership is narrow. This is a stagflation tape being priced through channel differentiation — tech absorbs the good‑inflation news, cyclicals absorb the good‑activity news, and the belly of the Treasury curve stays where the terminal rate is.

The thing to watchFriday’s US September NFP at 13:30 BST is the specific event that locks the December Fed probability. Reuters consensus is +100k from +162k; UR 4.2% from 4.1%. ADP’s +90k print skews the NFP setup to a modestly stronger read; a print above +110k with UR at 4.1% takes the 10Y through 5.30% and pushes some of the December odds even higher. A print below +70k with UR at 4.3% puts October back on the table and forces the belly to bulge for the first time in a fortnight. The setup remains skewed to the hot side, but the ADP and Chicago PMI beats today reduce the tail on the soft‑side surprise. Michigan’s 4.6% one‑year inflation expectations from Friday and the Conference Board’s explicit fuel‑cost call‑out remain the reason to lean slightly hawkish. And behind the payroll data is the payrolls‑methodology question: BLS’s benchmark revision is due in the November release, and the market has not yet priced how large it will be.

03UK GDP revised up to 0.5% — the Budget arithmetic just got easier, but sterling didn’t pay

The 0.1pp headline revision is small; the 4.4pp business‑investment revision is not. The Chancellor now has marginally more headroom into 28 October, and the market has not priced it.

The release. ONS at 07:00 BST published the final Q2 GDP estimate: +0.5% QoQ, revised up from a +0.4% third estimate and a +0.3% second estimate. YoY +2.0%, revised from +1.9%. The composition of the revision is the specific piece worth reading. Services output growth was 0.6% for the quarter, the primary driver of the revision, versus a previously estimated 0.4%. Manufacturing output was revised from −0.1% to +0.1%. Construction was unchanged. The individual sub‑series that mattered most: business investment was revised sharply up to +5.2% year‑on‑year from a preliminary +0.8%, and the quarterly reading came in at +1.8%. That is a 4.4pp upward revision on the single component of GDP that the OBR’s medium‑term forecast leans on hardest and that the Chancellor’s tax base modelling weights disproportionately.

What this actually changes for the Budget. The Chancellor’s 28 October event sits inside a fiscal frame that the market has been pricing off two data points for a fortnight: August borrowing at £3.5bn above the OBR path (bad), and the third estimate GDP at +0.4% (bearish for the base case). Today’s revision doesn’t solve the borrowing overshoot, but it does two things. First, it tells the OBR that its Q2 base for the medium‑term forecast was too pessimistic, which pushes the potential‑output line marginally upward and improves the cyclically‑adjusted deficit position. Second, it upgrades the business‑investment vector, which raises expected corporate tax receipts through 2027–28. The rough magnitude of the fiscal headroom created is on the order of £3–5bn on a static analysis — not enough to close the borrowing gap on its own, but material against the deficit adjustment the Chancellor has been signalling. Reeves now has fractionally more room to defer or reduce the tax package the market was pricing.

Why sterling didn’t pay. The pound briefly ran to the 1.3260s intraday on the release before giving it all back on the US ADP print and closing at 1.3224, essentially unchanged on Tuesday. The read is that FX is trading two things right now: (i) the transatlantic rate differential, where the ADP print firmed the December Fed hike case, and (ii) the fiscal signal into the Budget. The GDP revision is a slow‑burn positive that shows up over multiple sessions as OBR intern chatter, then Treasury committee testimony, then Chancellor Q&A. It does not print as a single‑session FX repricing. The single line to read is that cable now has less downside from the fiscal event than it did 24 hours ago. If the Budget lands with a smaller tax rise than the £30bn+ number the market has been carrying, sterling has a headroom rally back to 1.34 rather than sliding to 1.30. The upward revision has quietly capped the downside.

What this does to your book. The direct read on Fidelity European £15,912 is neutral — the fund is Continental European, so a UK growth revision doesn’t transmit through it. The dollar sleeve has less to worry about on the FX side than it did last week — if sterling is capped at 1.30 rather than heading through 1.28, the mechanical FX tailwind on your £93,000 dollar look‑through is smaller than the weekly review estimated. UKW is the one line where the Budget has direct read‑through: if the Chancellor pulls back on planned windfall‑style measures for renewables (which a stronger fiscal position makes easier), the discount narrows structurally. Nothing dated between now and 28 October in the pre‑Budget briefing calendar changes this materially, but keep watching Treasury committee readouts from next week.

The Budget frame that mattersThe market has been pricing a £30bn+ tax package into 28 October on the borrowing overshoot. Today’s revision gives roughly £3–5bn of static headroom relative to the OBR’s previous Q2 base — not enough to eliminate the tax rise, enough to shift it toward the lower end of consensus. The specific line the desk should watch through 28 October is 10‑year gilt yield: if that drifts down from its current ~5.35% area on Treasury signalling, it’s reading the same thing your brief is. UKW at 8,349 shares is the position that pays most for a benign Budget outcome, not through gilts alone but through the discount trade. It is also the position most exposed if the Chancellor delivers a windfall‑style measure and the tape treats renewables as a wallet.

Storage

EU aggregate storage 71% at the AGSI+ read for 30 September per MacroMicro, Voltstack and Consilium, marginally up from Tuesday’s 71.05%. Still at approximately +0.15pp/day pace, still tracking a third of what would be needed to reach 90% by 1 November. Aggregate now roughly 16–17pp below the five‑year seasonal norm. The EU winter‑fill target has been formally relaxed to 80% from the 90% baseline under the flexibility provisions of the Gas Storage Regulation — the Commission’s legal path opened last week and this week’s data confirmed the target could not be hit at 90%. Italy 87.0% and France 83.2% are already above the relaxed target; Germany 57.7% and Austria 68.1% are not.

Germany today ordered SEFE to inject 8 TWh into storage by 15 December. This is the material policy action of the week and it lands on the specific date the Gas Storage Regulation deadline slips through. SEFE is the former Gazprom Germania that Berlin nationalised in 2022; the injection order is executed at government direction, not market economics, and is funded through the Bundesnetzagentur allocation mechanism. The 8 TWh is roughly 2.7pp of aggregate German storage — a meaningful contribution to closing the gap versus the 80% relaxed target, and one that will show up in AGSI+ over the next 10‑12 weeks. The market will read this as a formal government commitment to underwrite the storage build; TTF should have had more of a rally than it did. Your desk read on why it didn’t is (i) LNG supply reaching Europe holding up, (ii) the Norway story softening as outages resolve, and (iii) Hormuz talks continuing to skim the risk premium.
