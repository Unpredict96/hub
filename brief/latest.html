02UK CPI 3.5% hot — gilts rallied anyway. The pre‑Budget bid is now explicit.

Headline 3.5% YoY vs 3.4% consensus (August 3.1%). Core 2.8% YoY vs 2.7% consensus (August 2.6%). Services 3.7% YoY vs 3.6% consensus (August 3.4%). All three measures printed hot; services, the Bank’s preferred measure, moved. The expected reaction — 30Y to 6%, sterling through 1.33 — did not happen. 30Y rallied 9bp to 5.79%; sterling steady at 1.3384; FTSE 100 up 0.19% to 10,752.88. The pre‑Budget bid absorbed the overshoot.

The specific CPI composition

Transport added more to the annual rate than any other group — motor fuels were up ~30% YoY as September’s Brent spike passed through the forecourt in real time. Goods inflation stepped up to 3.2% YoY from 2.7% in August; services at 3.7% YoY from 3.4%. The core measure accelerating to 2.8% — the first move above 2.6% since early 2026 — is the signal that the energy‑driven first‑round effects are now cascading into the second‑round services pipeline. September PPI output prices (same release) showed the pipeline: input price index up at the fastest annual pace since 2022, confirming that the October headline CPI print (released mid‑November) almost certainly stays above 3.5% and may reach 3.8%.

Why gilts rallied anyway: three mechanisms

The 30Y moved from 5.88% at Tuesday’s close to 5.79% by Wednesday’s London close — a 9bp rally into a 10bp consensus beat. The specific drivers:

  • The Bank of England’s own 4% peak projection is already priced. The Q4 2026 projection from the August MPR was ~3.75% average headline; the Q1 2027 projection sat >4%. The September 3.5% print puts the trajectory inside the Bank’s own forecast range — which the market has been pricing for six weeks. A hot print that merely confirms the trajectory is a non‑event; the market already positioned long duration going in.
  • The pre‑Budget bid is now explicit. The Budget on Wednesday 28 October is the single scheduled event the gilt market cares about through end‑October. Market expectation is a credible consolidation plan with fiscal headroom in the £10–12bn range, which de‑risks the November gilt issuance programme. Market positioning tilted long duration into the print; the hot CPI was not enough to flip that positioning before the Budget event risk is resolved.
  • The November BoE meeting is now pricing a 25bp hike with ~70% probability. The combination of hot CPI + the Bank’s already‑hawkish projection path means a hike is now the base case for 6 November. A hike that is already priced supports the long end on expectations of lower terminal‑rate dispersion — i.e. a Bank that moves decisively now reduces the risk premium that was being paid for “Bank behind the curve” drift. The curve steepened at the belly but the long end rallied.

Correcting the government framing

Yesterday’s brief (and the Saturday 17 October weekly review) carried “Reeves Budget” as the 28 October event. This is incorrect and has been incorrect since Starmer was replaced by Burnham on 20 July 2026, with Healey taking the Chancellorship. The structural implications are material for the Budget frame:

  • Burnham government priorities differ from Starmer / Reeves. Burnham’s policy platform leans more heavily on NHS funding commitments (specifically the ten‑year plan funding timetable), devolved planning reform, and climate transition / green industrial strategy. The implicit framing — which the past three weeks of briefs carried — of “Reeves consolidation with three protected lines (NHS / education / defence)” is obsolete.
  • Healey as Chancellor brings a different fiscal discipline question. Reuters polling puts his headroom at £10–12bn; the lower bound of the headroom range sat at £8.5bn as of mid‑October following gilt‑yield‑driven interest cost revisions. Market pricing: £10bn+ headroom plus credible consolidation plan lets 30Y back to 5.65–5.75%; sub‑£8bn headroom plus visible stealth taxation snaps 30Y back to 6.00% and sterling toward 1.32.
  • Healey publicly backed Heathrow third runway on 16 October — the first public policy departure from Burnham on an industrial‑strategy question. The specific signal to carry: Healey is positioning as the fiscal conservative within the Burnham government, which supports the credible‑consolidation reading at the Budget event.
