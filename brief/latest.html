01The five-minute version

Three things earned space today: Trump’s Saturday rejection of Iran’s seven-day Hormuz proposal, which took the tape apart in the first hour and it never fully recovered; Nvidia’s $150bn buyback authorisation into a red Nasdaq, taking the total programme to $235bn and printing NVDA against a broadly lower semi tape; and the specific piece of the bond move which was the 30-year holding Friday’s 5.5% break rather than reversing it.

One. Trump rejected Iran’s seven‑day proposal on Saturday, and the Monday tape gave back most of Friday’s Hormuz‑relief round trip.

Al Jazeera, Reuters and the CNBC oil desk all reported through the weekend and into Monday morning that Trump had told reporters on Saturday that Tehran’s latest proposal was “not acceptable”. Iran’s Foreign Ministry, through spokesman Baghaei on Sunday, said Tehran was “awaiting a definitive US response” and that it would not ease its conditions — specifically, the demand for a full lifting of the naval blockade and release of frozen Iranian funds in exchange for reopening the Strait within seven days. The rejection landed on a curve that had priced the Friday phased‑deal framework at essentially 40% probability of holding. Monday’s open unwound that in the first hour: Brent gapped from Friday’s $105.11 close to intraday high $108.83, WTI printed $94.55 intraday, and the November Brent contract touched $107.35 before US hours. The retrace was almost as violent: by 20:00 BST Brent was back at $105.28, a hair above Friday, with the day close reflecting a market that has repriced the tail but not moved the median. That intraday range — $3.55 top to close — is a fair proxy for the width of the Hormuz risk premium the curve now carries. It is not narrow.

Two. Nvidia authorised $150bn of additional buybacks and launched the Open Agent Safety Platform on a red tape, and printed +1.68% while AMD lost 3.66%.

The Nvidia announcement was the single most consequential company‑specific event of the day, and the framing is important. Nvidia’s board approved a $150bn increase to the share repurchase authorisation, taking the total programme to $235bn, the largest single buyback authorisation in corporate history, expected to run through fiscal year 2028. In parallel, Nvidia launched Openshell and Sentry — the two components of an Open Agent Safety Platform designed to restrict what AI agents can access and monitor their behaviour. Named partners: Anthropic, Cisco, CrowdStrike, Dell, Hugging Face, JPMorgan, Microsoft, Palantir, Salesforce, SAP, Scale AI, ServiceNow, and SpaceX. Nvidia’s framing of the launch pointed explicitly at the Hugging Face agent breach earlier this year (which the company argues Openshell would have prevented) — a specific reference to a specific incident that gives the announcement more weight than a generic safety press release. NVDA closed +1.68% at $228.86 into a Nasdaq that closed −0.92%. AMD — the name of last week — gave back 3.66% as the CPU trade reversed and profit was taken on the “Muse repriced silicon” framing. That gap between Nvidia and AMD in one session is the second‑most information‑dense data point of the day, because it argues that the merchant‑GPU incumbent still leads the tape when the sleeve corrects.

02Trump rejects Iran, the tape reprices in three hours

Saturday’s rejection landed on an oil market that had already priced Friday’s phased-deal framework at partial probability. The Monday open removed that pricing violently, then handed most of it back by the US close.

The sequence: Iran, through Foreign Minister Araghchi, proposed at UNGA on Friday a seven‑day framework in which the US would release frozen Iranian funds, lift sanctions, and end its naval blockade of Iranian ports, in exchange for Iran reopening the Strait of Hormuz and resuming nuclear negotiations. Trump was asked about the proposal at a press availability Saturday evening and said flatly that the offer was “not acceptable.” The specific piece of his framing was that the sequencing was wrong — the proposal did not front‑load Iranian action, so the US was being asked to move first. By Sunday afternoon, Iran’s Foreign Ministry spokesman Baghaei confirmed Tehran was “waiting for a definitive US response” and added — this is the tape‑moving line — that Iran would not ease its conditions.

The Monday reaction was a textbook Hormuz round trip. Brent opened Asian hours at $107.35 and pushed to $108.83 intraday in the European session. Sterling and the dollar strengthened together, an unusual co-move that says the risk-off was global, not US‑specific. Then the US open pulled the tape back: equity weakness, yields higher, and the belief that OPEC+ would deliver a “steady state” posture at Sunday’s meeting (see below) all combined to fade the intraday spike. Brent settled $105.28, a fraction above Friday’s $105.11. WTI settled roughly $93. The day’s range — call it $103.7 on the low, $108.83 on the high — is $5, which is a fair proxy for how wide the Hormuz option is trading right now.

The OPEC+ context is important because the meeting is next Sunday, 5 October. Delegate leaks through the weekend from Reuters, Bloomberg and Energy Intelligence continued to point to a “steady state” October production posture. The group is holding back on further quota increases pending a review of members’ production capacity to set 2027 baselines — a technical debate that runs into Q4. That means the market does not expect a specific supply‑side loosening from OPEC+ to counter a Hormuz escalation, which puts the entire burden of resolution on the diplomatic thread. If Trump’s posture hardens through this week and Iran’s does the same — both consistent with weekend statements — the market will begin to price the reverse of Friday: not a Hormuz relief, but a Hormuz freeze into winter. That is how you get Brent trading in a $105–$110 range without the tape needing new information.

The interesting piece to watchThe specific tell tomorrow is whether Iran issues a further concrete concession or hardens its language further. A concession — on the fund release timing, on the port blockade sequencing — keeps the framework alive and Brent trades back through $104. A hardening — explicit rejection of “US first” or a shipping-lane restriction — and Brent breaks $108 with intent. The trade for the moment is not directional; it is volatility. If you were a systematic vol‑seller on Brent, you would step aside this week.

03Nvidia’s $150bn answer to a bad tape

The largest buyback authorisation in corporate history, an agent-safety launch with fourteen enterprise partners, and a NVDA close of +1.68% into a −0.92% Nasdaq. The mega-cap that lagged the week ran the day.

The buyback headline. Nvidia’s board authorised a $150bn increase to the existing $85bn share‑repurchase programme, bringing the total remaining authorisation to $235bn, to run through fiscal 2028. For scale, this is the largest single buyback authorisation in corporate history — roughly double Alphabet’s $70bn increase from earlier in the year, and larger than Apple’s $110bn authorisation in 2024. On a market cap of roughly $5.5trn, the $235bn represents about 4.3% of shares outstanding at spot; over three fiscal years, that is meaningful even for a name buying back into a 30× forward multiple. Two things matter more than the notional. First, the timing: this authorisation was made public on the specific Monday when the CPU‑trade reversal was doing damage to the AMD line and there was no fundamental positive to counter it. It reads as an intentional signal that the company’s own capital‑allocation view is that the shares are cheap here. Second, the execution runway: FY28 ends in January 2029, which gives Nvidia three years to work the programme; if it executes at a pace comparable to the buyback rate implied by the trailing $85bn programme, the incremental annual buying is on the order of $60–80bn, which is enough to be a material technical support in the tape.

The Open Agent Safety Platform is the other half of the story. Openshell and Sentry are the two named components: Openshell is the sandboxed execution environment (restricts filesystem, network, and privileged calls that an AI agent can make); Sentry is the runtime monitor (observes behaviour and alerts on anomalies). The named partner list is unusual: Anthropic, Cisco, CrowdStrike, Dell, Hugging Face, JPMorgan Chase, Microsoft, Palantir, Salesforce, SAP, Scale AI, ServiceNow, SpaceX. Two of those are the specific enterprise names that had public agent‑security incidents earlier in 2026 (the Hugging Face agent breach; the Salesforce agent‑impersonation story), and Nvidia’s framing explicitly names the Hugging Face incident as one Openshell would have prevented. This is a competitive product move against the emerging “agent‑security‑as‑an‑independent‑company” category (Irregular, which broke the Gemini vulnerability a fortnight ago, is not on the partner list — a pointed omission). Sell‑side coverage this evening frames it as Nvidia moving up the stack from silicon into agent‑infrastructure‑middleware, which is the layer where OpenAI, Anthropic and Microsoft have been building their moats.

The tape confirmed the framing. NVDA closed +1.68% at $228.86. That is a meaningful outperformance against a Nasdaq that closed −0.92%, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index which was down more than that, and AMD which lost 3.66% on the day. Read for you: Nvidia is now $228.86 versus Friday’s $225.00 — a +1.7% move on 2.17 shares, worth about £9 direct and roughly +£90 across the £5,300 look‑through. Against the AMD reversal (−3.66% on ~£1,300 look‑through = ~−£40) and against a broadly softer tech tape into Nasdaq −0.92%, the net on the sleeve is a smaller negative than a naive Nasdaq‑multiplier would give you. Roughly −£100 to −£150 across the mid‑six US megacaps look‑through, offset by the NVDA leg. Meta and Alphabet Monday closes were not verified; the sector‑wide read suggests both were red by roughly the Nasdaq magnitude.

The read for the AMD-versus-NVDA tradeThe Muse trade that repriced CPUs last week did not survive its first Monday. AMD −3.66% while NVDA +1.68% is the reverse of last Monday’s +8.9% AMD move that took it briefly through the $1trn tag. The trade at the sleeve level was “CPUs are the surprise beneficiary of agent orchestration”; today’s read is “GPU incumbency and enterprise agent‑safety are the durable moats.” This is not a repudiation of the AMD thesis — the EPYC pricing power at Nebius, the Muse architecture running on CPUs, none of that has changed. It is the market rejecting the pace at which the trade was extrapolated. AMD Q3 earnings on 3 November is where the thesis becomes measurable rather than notional.
