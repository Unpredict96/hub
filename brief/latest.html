On Thursday the question was governance against earnings. Since then the governance question has become political, a regulatory cost has been added, and the stock has fallen another 6% before the print.

What is new since Thursday

The board has ruled out a return. CNBC’s source says the board is “not discussing” bringing Huang back. Trump said on Monday morning that he supported a return, and Bessent said over the weekend that he wanted a “dramatic shakeup” and an outside hire. Thursday’s brief treated the governance question as mostly settled once Huang left. It is not settled. The administration now has stated views on who should run the company, and that company sits at the centre of US export-control policy. WSJ also reported that the administration plans to name Nvidia and other AI firms “national champions”, with no details. Together these mean the CEO search will be judged partly on politics. That is a risk Thursday’s scenario table did not price.

The BIS rule. Commerce issued an interim final rule that closes the loophole letting Chinese firms rent Nvidia compute in data centres in other countries. Earlier reporting in May and July described this rule being drafted, with Thailand and Singapore named. Export-control lawyers have said BIS lacks clear statutory authority over remote access until the Senate passes the Remote Access Security Act, which the House passed 369–22 in January. Expect the rule to be challenged on those grounds. For tomorrow, the question is whether Kress quantifies what share of data-centre revenue came from Chinese-parented customers renting capacity offshore. If that share is material, the Q4 guide has to absorb it.

Consensus for tomorrow. The company guided Q3 revenue to $108bn ±2% at a 74% ±0.5pt gross margin. Consensus sits a little above the guide: Visible Alpha $109.3bn, LSEG about $109.2bn, Chartmill $111.2bn. EPS consensus is $2.47–2.52 and the whisper number is $2.60. Data-centre consensus is about $101.8bn. One source puts the options-implied move at about ±10.6%, measured on 10 November. I have only that one source, and the implied move has probably risen since. On today’s close, ±10.6% is a range of about $140 to $173.

How the exit rule reads tonight

Thursday’s rule was to sell the line if Kress withdraws the FY28 framing or Q3 revenue misses by more than 200bp. A 200bp miss against the $108bn guide means revenue below about $105.8bn. That is the right test of the business and I am not changing it a day before the print. Its weakness is that it does not cover the most likely bad outcome: an in-line quarter with a Q4 guide trimmed for China rental demand, followed by a 7–10% fall on the guide. If that happens, the stock goes through Wolfe’s $150 support and the next level in their work is about $100. I would add one condition for Tuesday night: if the Q4 revenue guide comes in below Q3 actual revenue, treat it as equivalent to withdrawing the framing. A sequential decline in guidance would be the first since the AI cycle began, and it would change how the stock is valued. You may prefer to keep the original rule. In that case, decide before 22:00 GMT tomorrow rather than during the after-hours move.

What I cannot tell you is the size of the position. Without the share count I cannot say whether a 10% move is £150 or £1,500. It is the most important missing input in the book tonight.

The read-across to the rest of the sleeve

ASML (−5.91%) and TSM (−6.34%) both fell more than NVDA. ASML has now given back its whole gain since entry, going from +7.7% on Thursday’s figure to −1.2%. TSM has fallen three sessions in a row from $413.35 to $373.45, which is −9.7%. When the suppliers fall more than the company everyone is watching, the market is selling the capex cycle, not one company’s governance. A good Nvidia number tomorrow should help these two more than it helps NVDA, because their discount is about capex and NVDA’s is partly about governance. A weak guide hurts all of them. GEV’s −4.93% says the market treats it as part of the AI power-demand group, which makes it less of a diversifier inside the sleeve than its “grid” label suggests.

03Oil: Hormuz flows return, and Shell’s $90 leg tightens

Mine headlines continue, but the barrels are moving through the Strait. The market is trading the flows. That is the scenario most likely to put Brent below $90.

What the tape did

  • Friday 13 November: Brent settled at about $95.84 (Monday’s −$1.67 move implies this), up from about $94.5 on Thursday. The energy sector rose more than 5% on the week, its best since mid-August.
  • Monday 16 November: about $95.3 at 07:00 GMT (Proactive), $96.13 (+0.3%) mid-morning on the IRGC mine claims (CNBC), $94.17 (−1.74%) at 14:10 GMT (Reuters), and about $92 late (Yahoo). WTI December closed at $85.07–85.53, down 3.4–3.9%. On the late print the day’s range was about $4.

Why it fell

Flows. Reuters reported Hormuz shipments “nearly restored to pre-war levels”. Before the war, about 15 mb/d of crude passed through the Strait (IEA, 2025). A third US carrier has arrived in the region. Iran says it is mining the Strait while tankers keep transiting it, which suggests the tankers are being escorted, insured or routed around the mines. The flows are what affect the physical balance.

The IEA. The Friday Oil Market Report moved from a Q4 deficit to a 1.7 mb/d surplus, with supply at 105 mb/d. It also said 71 mb from March’s record stock release, including 15 mb of diesel, will be available by the end of February. That diesel matters for the Shell thesis because it limits how high the diesel crack can go in Q1.

China. October refinery runs fell 2% month on month and 8.1% year on year. Reuters estimates that implies crude stocks building at 492 kb/d, with the barrels going into storage rather than being refined. China’s October activity data missed estimates across the board.

Diplomacy, not confirmed. Over the weekend there were reports of a Qatari-brokered interim deal. Under it, Iran would reopen Hormuz and stop tanker attacks, and the US would lift its blockade and release $8bn of frozen funds held in Qatar, with Qatar’s prime minister carrying the proposal to the White House. Two diplomatic sources told Israeli media this; neither government has confirmed it. Rubio called Iran’s demands “outrageous and ridiculous”. Bessent, Lutnick, Rubio and Hegseth are reported to oppose terms like these. Trump said further strikes are “highly unlikely” if talks produce a strong agreement. That is a softer line than Thursday’s “we’ve got their Strait”. A similar memorandum agreed in Switzerland in May collapsed after a tanker attack.

04Pill, the 2-year above 5%, and a weak pound

The UK rates story is hikes, not a Budget. Wednesday’s CPI is the BoE’s last major input before 17 December.

What Pill said. In a weekend interview with the FT, the chief economist called the November hike to 4.00% a “significant step”. He said further increases remain possible and that market pricing is “not outrageous”. Coming from the MPC’s most hawkish member, that reads as approval of market pricing, and the market acted on it. The 2-year gilt touched 5.015% intraday, its highest since October 2023. December hike pricing went to about 91% from 86% on Friday. Finimize had 92% at a different time.

What I could not verify. I could not get a Monday close for the 10-year gilt. Thursday’s brief carried 5.20%. Late-October reporting around the Budget put it at 5.32–5.40%, which doesn’t fit with 5.20% on 12 November unless gilts rallied about 15bp in between. They might have: the DMO cut long-dated issuance in the Budget. I am flagging the 10-year rather than guessing, because it anchors the UKW discount-to-NAV argument.

Sterling. GBP/USD went to about 1.3185–1.3210 in European trading, its sixth straight daily loss and a four-week low. The pound fell even with UK 2-year yields rising, because the dollar is gaining on Fed hike pricing and a 10-year Treasury of about 5.1%. For your book, every 1% fall in the pound adds about 1% to the sterling value of the US lines: BRK-B, DIDIY, the six T212 ISA names and the US part of the funds. On a day like today, that cushions the sterling loss slightly.

The Budget correction. As set out in the alert above, the Autumn Budget was delivered on 28 October by Chancellor John Healey. Reporting at the time described a muted gilt reaction, with the DMO cutting planned long-dated issuance, and the pound heading for its first monthly gain since August. Recent pieces described a coming “Budget duration event” for UKW and GSK. There isn’t one. The UK duration events now are UK CPI on Wednesday 18 November at 07:00, the BoE on Thursday 17 December, and the gilt supply calendar. That includes the DMO syndication window that last week’s review placed on 18 November; I have not reconfirmed its date.

US rates. The Empire State survey fell 18 points to 7.8 against 16.7 expected, with new orders down to 3.0. Prices paid fell to 45.9 from 59.5, and employment rose to 18.1, the highest since June 2022. Lower price pressure is good news for the Fed. Strong hiring is not. Yahoo’s ticker had the 10-year Treasury at 5.12%. If Thursday’s 4.93% was right, yields rose 19bp in two sessions while equities fell, which would be a meaningful tightening. Tuesday’s October retail sales at 13:30 GMT is the next data point.

05Gas and LNG desk view

TTF gave back part of Thursday’s spike on Friday. JKM now trades above TTF, so marginal flexible cargoes have an incentive to go to Asia. Storage is the lowest for the date on record.

Prices

TTF front-month: Friday 13 November closed at about €90.2–90.8/MWh. EnergyRiskIQ has €90.23 (−0.61% on its prior print) as the last close, and another page has €90.78 (−4.04%) on 14 November. Against Thursday’s €93.45, Friday was down about 3%. I have no verified Monday settle. The week-on-week change is about +3.4% and the 30-day change about +2.0%. The October peak was above €101, the highest since 2022, before peace-talk pricing took it down to €85.59 by 6 November. The Shell leg 1 trigger at €60 is about 50% below the price.

JKM: about $31.35/MMBtu (EnergyRiskIQ). At an assumed EUR/USD of 1.15, which I have not verified today, TTF €90.2 converts to about $30.4/MMBtu. Asia is paying about $1/MMBtu more than Europe, enough to cover the extra freight for marginal US cargoes in a normal market. That is today’s most useful observation. Europe is not attracting flexible LNG at the margin even though its storage is at a record low for the date. Either TTF has to rise to pull cargoes back, or the storage draw speeds up. Your freight and arb screens will show which.

NBP and UK power: I have no verified NBP day-ahead or front-month figure for Friday or Monday. Thursday’s brief had about 225–230p/therm front-month. If TTF fell 3% on Friday, NBP probably did too, unless UK-specific tightness held it up. I have nothing on that either way. UK Winter baseload power was also not verified today.

Storage

  • EU aggregate: 70.5–70.8% (13–15 November; EnergyRiskIQ quoting AGSI+). That is about 18 points below the seasonal norm of 88.5%. ENGIE calls it the lowest for the date in the 2017–2025 range, and other reporting calls it the lowest since records began in 2009. A year ago it was about 82.4%, per Thursday’s brief.
  • Germany: 57.5% on 13 November, down from 58.0% on 10 November and 61.17% on 1 November, so about 0.3 points a day. One tracker says it is the lowest for the date since 2011. At that rate Germany reaches about 47% by mid-December. A sustained cold spell could double the rate, which would put it near 35% by Christmas.
  • Italy: Thursday’s figure was about 91%. Not re-verified today. Italy is the comfortable side of the gap between countries.
  • UK: aggregate storage was about 40% per Thursday; Rough was at 0.0 TWh and paused. Neither was re-verified today. UK storage is tiny compared with the continent. The UK relies on LNG sendout and Norwegian flows, which is why the JKM premium matters more for NBP than for TTF.

Supply

  • Qatar: force majeure is extended to the end of December (29 December per last week’s pieces). About 17% of export capacity is damaged, and repairs to two trains take about three years. The undamaged trains could restart within weeks of a Hormuz settlement. The damaged ones cannot, regardless of diplomacy. I have no list of buyers, cargo counts or months affected. If a Qatari interim deal reopens the Strait, the undamaged trains are the bearish risk for TTF in January.
  • Norway: I have no Gassco nomination data or maintenance volumes for this week. Reporting cites “several unplanned outages”. The Visund fire of 4–5 November has no confirmed return date in anything I can see. One forecast says Norway’s 2027 maintenance programme is about 20% heavier than 2026 and concentrated in the autumn refill window. That matters for next winter’s refill, not this one.
  • Hormuz and LNG: Iran says a gas tanker hit a mine over the weekend. This is the first LNG-carrier claim I have seen in this run of incidents, and it is unverified. If it is confirmed, war-risk cover for LNG carriers through the Strait will be repriced, which matters for the restart of Qatar’s undamaged trains.
  • UK LNG sendout: not verified. Isle of Grain, South Hook and Dragon have had no Qatari supply since the force majeure.
  • Freight: not verified. The JKM-over-TTF spread suggests the US–Asia route pays better than US–Europe on a delivered basis. That reverses only if Panama transit or Cape freight costs eat the $1.

Policy

Germany’s strategic gas reserve announcement (11 November) is unchanged. The EU Energy Council meets in the week of 1 December, with Germany’s 4% storage-flexibility request on the agenda. The UK WCfD consultation closes Wednesday 9 December, and UKW’s opt-in announcement is still outstanding. I found no IEA or Commission intervention today.
