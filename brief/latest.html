01The five-minute version

One central bank, one CPI print, one triple-witch, one retail-sales beat. The book carries.

One. The BoJ delivered the hike. The yen fell four full figures anyway.

Ueda took the policy rate to 1.25% on a 7–2 vote, the highest since 1995, with board members Asada and Sato dissenting for a hold. Every strand of the market had priced this at 100% coming in. What no-one had priced was the split — consensus was for 8–1 or 9–0 — and Ueda’s post-decision line that “we don’t assume a specific pace for further interest rate hikes.” That is the second time in three meetings the BoJ has hiked without commitment to a follow-up. USD/JPY ran to 157.07 intraday and closed near 156.95, +0.63% on the day and back near the levels that have historically preceded Ministry of Finance intervention. The read is clean: markets priced the hike; markets bought the dovish tone.

Two. US ten-year yield jumped back over 5%.

The three-basis-point rally on Thursday completely reversed. The 10-year climbed more than five basis points to 5.004%, pushing back through the round number and setting up the worst week for the Dow since March. The story is the same as it was Wednesday night: the market cannot decide whether to price Warsh’s hawkish tone at face value or discount for the possibility Powell talks him down. Today it discounted less. The Dow shed 95 points to 51,682, the S&P closed +0.17% at 7,650.50 — a small green day that masks a red week for the widest-followed index. Nasdaq +0.39% to 26,522 fared a touch better on the mega-cap-tech bid.

Three. The semi rally reversed in the biggest name that matters to you.

SOXX +3% on Thursday was the day that made yesterday’s brief say the ATT trim was “marginally the wrong call for the first time.” Today NVIDIA closed −1.5% at $217.44 and AMD closed −2.36% at $459.61. The other AI-adjacent megacaps did not follow: Microsoft +1.5%, Apple +1.4%, Alphabet +1.3%, Meta +1.3%. That is the split you want if you are worried about NVIDIA-specific concentration — the sector-composite bounce did not carry the largest chip stocks a second day, but the broad tech complex is still supported by rate-relief mechanics on the software side.

And a UK data point that actually surprised.

ONS August retail sales came in +0.5% MoM against a Bloomberg consensus of −0.2%. Warm weather, non-store recovery, and a food-stores bounce. Rolling three-month volumes +0.9%. That is the second consumer print in two months (August payrolls beat, this) telling the November MPC there is no visible demand cooling that would let the doves win the argument. It sits in the same box as Warsh’s labour-market frame from Wednesday: the actual data is not delivering the softness the doves need.

02The BoJ hike that weakened the yen

A 7–2 vote when consensus wanted 8–1, and a governor who refused to name a pace.

Break the decision apart and every piece except the headline is dovish.

The vote count was softer than expected. The Reuters/Bloomberg consensus going in was 8–1 or 9–0 for the hike; the print was 7–2, with dissents from Toichiro Asada and Ayano Sato. Asada is a long-standing dove for whom a hold is unsurprising. Sato is not, and her switch to the hold side is the more consequential of the two dissents — it is a market read that the internal ballot has more resistance to further tightening than the aggregate track record would suggest. Two dissenters against a 25bp move that had 100% market probability going in is the kind of split that sows doubt about what a January or March hike would look like.

Ueda’s language did not commit to more. The quoted line was “we don’t assume a specific pace for further interest rate hikes,” matched with framing that today’s move was in response to upward inflation risk rather than a claim that inflation is running out of control. That is the same posture Ueda took at the July hike. In effect, the BoJ has now telegraphed a stop-start hiking cadence — one move at a time, on a case-by-case basis, with no forward guidance the FX market can price. The rate-differential trade against the dollar is now a Fed-story-only trade until the BoJ signals otherwise.

Japan August CPI came in below consensus. Core CPI (ex-fresh food) rose 1.7% y/y in August versus 1.8% expected and 1.8% in July — the first slowdown in four months. Headline held at 1.9% y/y. Ex-fresh-food-and-energy also held at 1.9%. Government energy-burden relief pushed energy back into negative territory, which is what did most of the work. Below-target on the BoJ’s preferred core measure gives the doves genuine air cover for a hold at the October meeting. The BoJ’s own communication frames today’s hike as a risk-management move against upward inflation, which is exactly the flavour of language that leaves the door open to a pause immediately.

Sterling’s cross with the yen is where you are indirectly exposed. You hold nothing Japanese, but the carry trade sits at the plumbing of every global fund allocator. A hiked BoJ raises the funding-leg cost of borrowing yen to buy dollar-denominated tech, which is one of the mechanical drivers behind the 30% AI-composite rally you own an ~£8,000 slice of. In practice, this hike is small enough — and Ueda’s tone dovish enough — that the carry-unwind risk that was the main downside case coming in is now off the table for the October window. The market thinks a January hike is now more probable than an October hike; funding cost stays low; the carry stays cheap.

The one place this bites is the Ministry of Finance intervention risk. USD/JPY at 157 puts you in the zone where MoF verbal intervention becomes daily and physical intervention becomes credible. Bessent has been pressing publicly for “decisive” steps on the weak yen. A physical intervention on the pair — a $50–100bn dollar-selling operation — is the sort of thing that can move risk assets globally for a few sessions on carry unwind. That is a tail scenario for now, not a base case, but it is the specific event to watch through the last week of September.

What this leaves for the rest of the yearThe BoJ is now visibly out of step with its own consensus-implied trajectory. Every economist who called this hike also called an October follow-up; today’s split and Ueda’s language argue for a December-earliest and a January-more-likely follow-up. That is a slower cycle than the market was writing, and it is why USD/JPY did not rally on the announcement. Your indirect exposure is limited to the funding-leg effect on carry-financed tech, and today’s outcome is on balance mildly bullish for AI-composite risk into the October Q3 reporting season.

03Ten-year back above 5%, gilts held

Thursday’s Warsh-follow-through bounce reversed. The US bond market is undecided; the UK bond market is not.

Yesterday’s brief flagged the ten-year rally at 4.95% as a Warsh-reassured tape; today the market took that back. The 10-year Treasury climbed more than five basis points to 5.004%, the 2-year firmed, and the Dow gave back Thursday’s gains and then some. The final tally: worst week for the Dow since March, S&P down ~0.3% on the week despite today’s +0.17%, Nasdaq marginally up on the week. That is a market that has spent five sessions unable to settle on whether the Fed’s next move is bark or bite.

What actually happened today was mostly triple witching. Roughly $7 trillion of US equity options expired at the close — Citadel Securities’ count puts it at ~25% of total US options exposure, the second-largest expiration on record. Positioning cleared, dealers hedged, and the equity tape traded fine through the settle. The bond move is the more instructive one. A five-basis-point back-up on a day where nothing new was said by any Fed speaker is a market re-pricing the terminal on its own reflection, not on new information. It suggests the base case going into the December FOMC has quietly moved from “60–70% priced” toward the upper end of that range.

Michelle Bowman spoke this morning at the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City. The speech was almost entirely on stress-test reform: a date for freezing bank balance sheets, two global market shock scenarios with the worse result feeding the stress-capital buffer, a timeline to finalise within weeks. Nothing on monetary policy. The market treated it as expected. Bowman’s stress-test agenda is running in parallel to the monetary policy narrative — it is a regulatory pipeline story that matters to bank equities, not to the front end of the curve.

The UK curve held today. UK 10-year gilt yield closed the day at roughly 5.25% per the Trading Economics tape, versus 5.34% at Thursday’s close and 5.41% before the BoE decision. The QT rewrite has kept the price of the long end — the housing sector held its Thursday gains, the mid-cap complex stayed steady, and Greencoat UK Wind added another ~1.5%. That is exactly what you would expect from a market that reads the QT slowdown as more persistent than any single rate decision. FTSE 100 gave back most of Thursday’s 1%-plus bounce on banks-and-energy weakness — oil down, sterling still weak, and the BoE’s hawkish lean weighing on UK-domestic rate-sensitive banks. FTSE 250 held broadly steady.

US industrial production came in unchanged for August against consensus of +0.3%, and capacity utilisation held at 76.3% against 76.4% consensus. Manufacturing output actually fell 0.3%. That is a mild disappointment on the goods side and does the work the labour market has been failing to do: it introduces a slowdown signal into the September activity read. Not enough to move the December Fed on its own, but enough to matter as part of a pattern if the September ISM prints (out 1 October) confirm the direction. This is the sort of data that lets the doves argue “the hike is working; wait.”

Two useful data details underneath the headline: computer-and-electronic-product capacity rose to 77.2% from 76.2%, and electrical-equipment capacity rose to 86.6% from 85.5%. Both are directly tied to AI infrastructure buildout. On a day where the semi-equity complex was mixed at best, the industrial-capacity data was quietly telling you the actual physical build has not slowed — and that is the datapoint the AI-composite bull case actually needs to keep working, more than any given hyperscaler’s Q4 capex commentary.

Where this leaves the sequenceThe December Fed is priced at roughly 65–70%. Two more months of NFP and CPI to route before then. The market is still trading the tone rather than the data on any given session, which is why the ten-year has yo-yoed 15bp inside 5.00% over the last five sessions without anything fundamental moving. The next event large enough to reset the trend is US September CPI on 15 October — three days after your own book’s major-catalyst window (Nvidia investor communications through the autumn, TSMC prints on 20 October). The tape has three quiet weeks ahead of that.

04The energy complex

Brent below $104 for the second consecutive session. Not a resolution, an unwinding.

Brent settled around $103.29, down 1.46% on the day and now over 5% below Monday’s intraday peak. The catalyst was the same as yesterday: additional Saudi crude routing via Hormuz plus hopes of renewed diplomatic activity around the strait. Nothing that resolves the underlying disruption, but enough to say the marginal physical shortage in the crude complex has been priced. Two consecutive down days of ~1.5% each after a five-week rally is a normal top-drift pattern; it becomes a genuine reversal if crude prints below $100 by early next week.

The refined-product story that turned $100 crude into a 23% motor-fuel CPI reading is still what needs to fade for the transmission to reverse. There is nothing today that shifts the ~800,000 b/d of Russian diesel loss or the ~1.2 mb/d of Hormuz-disrupted refined products. The combined idle refinery capacity is still ~5 mb/d. What this week gives you is a two-day easing of crude-side pressure without an easing of the distillate crack. On a two-week lag, UK pump prices see the marginal benefit; the October CPI print (21 October) is where you should first see the fuel line begin to moderate.

The Hormuz flow is stable near 7 mb/d against ~20 mb/d pre-conflict. Iranian permission still required, Tehran still evaluating the service fee. What has changed since June is the market’s ability to hold this state without a fresh price shock — that is what today’s $103 print really tells you. A steady-state degraded configuration is now the baseline, and the volatility premium has come out of the near curve. That is why Shell can be down $80 off Wednesday’s high without triggering a wider risk-off in the FTSE 100.

For your Shell position, the London close today at approximately 3,570–3,576p versus 3,652p on Wednesday’s close puts the position at ~£6,720–6,750, giving back roughly the £100–130 flagged in yesterday’s brief. The five-week rally from ~3,050p to 3,652p was 20%; the ~2% giveback since Wednesday is exactly on the beta the position was originally sized around. Not a thesis break, and Shell’s Q3 (late October) will still capture the average crude at ~$106 through the quarter, not the marginal $103 now. The dividend and buyback economics are unchanged.

The Fidelity European positioning gets a mild positive today. TTF steady ~€78 removes some cost pressure from the industrial book (ASML, Schneider, Roche in the top holdings). DAX +0.77% and CAC 40 +0.57% closes; sterling +30bp of the day’s move helps the EUR translation. This is the trade that quietly worked through the last two weeks and is still working — the fund is +9.7% on cost, unchanged from mid-week, and the fundamentals underneath it are strengthening rather than weakening.

05Gas and LNG desk view

Standing section. Your own screens beat everything below.

Prices

TTF front-month traded around €78.50/MWh late in the day, up ~2% on the session but still down about 1.8% for the week — the first weekly decline in six. Range earlier in the day was €78–79. That takes TTF ~€6 off Monday’s €84 intraday high and about €3 off Wednesday’s €81 print. The fade has been orderly and hub liquidity has held through it, which is what you want to see in a market that has just tested the highest levels since December 2022 and not broken anything on the way back down.

NBP day-ahead sits at 198.64p/therm on the last public read, up 4.15% on the day. That is a divergence from TTF direction — TTF slightly firmer but net weekly lower, NBP firmer and outpacing the continent. The mechanical driver is the same as it was in reverse two days ago: a shift in UK-domestic wind output. Wind was strong Thursday, prompt power fell 35% on the session; today wind eased and the prompt clawed back. NBP’s year-on-year comparison is still elevated but the week-on-week direction has flipped through this session. Your Argus screen will show this with more precision than the public trackers.

The TTF/NBP relationship that I flagged Monday as closed reopened today. TTF held/slightly up, NBP up 4%. That is a flow-vs-stock divergence in the UK direction — when the marginal domestic supply moves, NBP moves with it and the continent does not. This is what makes NBP a poor near-term hedge for European gas cost exposure and a good instrument for expressing views on UK weather and wind resource.

Storage

EU aggregate storage was ~68.66% full at the last AGSI+ read (17 September, 6 AM CEST). That is around 22 percentage points below the five-year seasonal norm at this point in the injection cycle. The country dispersion picture is broadly unchanged this week: Germany still at approximately 50–56% (the last verified public read for Germany was 50.43% in late August; the more current AGSI feed is what your screens will show), Italy still ~84%. Germany’s 80% mandatory storage target for winter 2026 (relaxed from 90%) remains in force, but with six weeks of injection season left and the trajectory as it stands, hitting even the 80% mark requires sustained near-capacity injection through October.

UK storage was around 49% at the last public read, unchanged. Rough remains empty. Centrica’s £2bn ask for regulatory-model inclusion still has no visible resolution.

Supply

  • Norway. Gassco this morning raised the impact of the Ormen Lange unplanned shutdown to 14.9 mcm/d from 12.9 mcm/d, and the earlier flagged outage — caused by a subsea compressor station failure — has now been extended to 1 February 2027 from the previous October end-date. That is a materially bearish shift in dated Norwegian supply, and it is arguably the single most important development in this section today. It removes the largest single dated bullish catalyst on the fade side of the curve; the ~24.5 mcm/d Ormen Lange return I flagged Monday for early October is now not happening. Karsto’s 12.6 mcm/d restart from yesterday is in and priced. If the Ormen Lange extension is confirmed on your Gassco feed, the net Norwegian supply trajectory into winter is meaningfully worse than the €77 TTF print appears to imply, and TTF’s fade this week may prove premature.
  • Qatar. QatarEnergy force majeure on Edison extended through end-September, unchanged from yesterday. 24 cargoes affected (~3 bcm), 17 replaced by Edison (~1.6 bcm), no new information on the restart of the 12 undamaged Ras Laffan trains. The 3–5 year rebuild timeline on the two damaged trains still stands. Pakistani, Bangladeshi and other European buyers on the same window.
  • LNG into the UK. No public update on sendout past the 10 September number of ~8 mcm/d. Your Kpler read is the definitive source.
  • Freight. Still no fresh public number on charter rates. Yesterday’s ask stands.

The policy layer

Nothing new in the last 24 hours. The Commission’s next public statement on winter preparedness remains the meaningful policy event still ahead, expected by end-September. The Oxford Institute for Energy Studies published a working note on refill economics that is worth reading if your Argus queue has slack — the key finding is that closing the current 22-percentage-point storage gap to the seasonal norm would require sustained LNG imports about 15% above the current run-rate through October, which is not physically deliverable given the Qatar disruption.

Where your data beats mine, ranked (1) Your Gassco read on the Ormen Lange extension to 1 February 2027. If confirmed, this is the material bullish input to any Q4 TTF view and it belongs in your Argus feed before it belongs anywhere else. (2) Your Kpler read on European LNG receipts over the past week — the 8.1 mcm/d UK sendout number is now over a week stale and the direction with TTF fading is the definitive tell. (3) Your desk’s German injection-rate print for this week. 55% at mid-September clears winter only with sustained near-capacity injection through October; the Ormen Lange extension makes that materially harder. (4) Charter rates. The March $161,750 figure is now six months stale; if Atlantic rates have collapsed with US exports normalising, the US–Europe versus US–Asia arb is what tells you whether the Kpler receipts number rises or falls next month.
