This settles last night’s top question. My two sources implied either about 0.25 or 0.45 points a day. The answer is faster than both. The EU went from 68.4% (23 November) to 66.6% (26 November), about 0.6 points a day. At roughly 1,130 TWh of working capacity, Energyflux’s 7.1 TWh/d on 25 November is 0.63 points, which matches. If draws average 0.5 points a day from here, the EU is at about 59% on 10 December and about 49% by New Year. That would be roughly where it ended in a normal February, five to six weeks early. For Germany, at the week’s average of 0.46 points a day, mid-December is about 46%. INES’s point that withdrawal capacity falls with fill starts to bind in the 40s, especially in the porous storage that dominates the German south.

So why did price fall? I see three reasons, and only the first is durable. (1) LNG competition has turned Europe’s way: the arb is shut to Asia in every month and JKM is at a widening discount, so Europe gets the marginal Atlantic cargo without having to outbid anyone. (2) Norway’s return (about 15 mcm/d to the continent from Thursday, per Serbia-Energy) removes the near-term supply fear. (3) Positioning: Monday’s €96 was a squeeze high, and a market that ran 12% higher on one cold forecast has room to give it back on a slightly less cold one. Against that, Europe imported 2.41 mt of LNG over the last four weeks against 2.74 mt a year ago. The arb is open, but volumes aren’t yet higher. Middle East LNG exports are at about 38% of pre-war pace on one count, and Ras Laffan loaded about 1.4 cargoes a day in November against 2.85 in February. The supply story is relative, not absolute.

My read. December is fine to trade lower: the cold is forecast to break after about 10 December, and the arb is open. Q1 is where the storage arithmetic arrives. The CNBC piece from last Friday describes a record-early stratospheric polar-vortex breakdown alongside a strong El Niño. Wood Mackenzie’s Lewis says the cold could land in December–January, and Rabobank’s Schmit says that at this withdrawal pace, prices spike “with weather rather than geopolitics as the driver”. I can’t see the curve tonight, so I can’t tell you whether Q1-27 sold off as much as the prompt. That is the most useful thing on your screens tonight (list below).
